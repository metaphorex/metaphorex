## What It Brings

When you act, you move yourself. This metaphor is the singular, system-level
statement of the mapping between volitional motion and intentional action
within Lakoff and Johnson's Event Structure metaphor. Where ACTIONS ARE
SELF-PROPELLED MOTIONS documents the general pattern across many expressions,
this entry names the specific slot in the Event Structure system: states are
locations, changes are movements, causes are forces, purposes are
destinations, and action -- the thing you do on purpose -- is self-propelled
motion.

The mapping's place in the system is what matters. The Event Structure
metaphor is a coherent network where each slot entails the others. If
states are locations and changes are movements, then causing a change is
applying a force, and acting -- changing things on purpose -- is generating
your own movement. ACTION IS SELF-PROPELLED MOTION is not just a metaphor
in isolation; it is a load-bearing component of a metaphorical system.

Key structural parallels:

- **Agency is self-propulsion** -- the critical feature is that the mover
  generates the motion from within. No one pushes you; you push yourself.
  This maps onto the core of human agency: you are the author of your
  action, not merely the site where it occurs.

- **Acting without external cause** -- "She moved on her own initiative."
  "He took the first step." "They set out independently." Self-propelled
  motion is motion without an external mover, which maps onto acting
  without an external cause. This is how the metaphor encodes free will:
  self-propulsion is the bodily prototype of autonomous choice.

- **Effort as exertion** -- "It took everything she had to keep going."
  "He pushed himself to finish." Self-propulsion requires energy from the
  agent, which maps onto the effort required for deliberate action. The
  harder the action, the more strenuous the self-propulsion.

- **Cessation as choosing to stop** -- "She stopped pursuing it." "He
  pulled back." In self-propelled motion, you can stop whenever you
  decide to. This maps onto the voluntariness of action: just as you
  chose to start, you can choose to cease.

## Where It Breaks

- **The metaphor conflates willingness with ability** -- self-propulsion
  implies that if you want to move, you can. But many desired actions are
  blocked not by lack of will but by lack of capacity, resources, or
  permission. The metaphor makes inability look like unwillingness:
  "Why don't you just do it?" assumes the only missing ingredient is
  the decision to start moving.

- **Compulsive and habitual action disappears** -- if action is
  self-propelled, then all action is voluntary. But addiction, compulsion,
  habit, and social pressure produce actions that the agent experiences as
  involuntary. The metaphor has no room for "I moved but I didn't
  propel myself" -- action that happens through you rather than by you.

- **Structural constraints are invisible** -- self-propelled motion
  happens in open space. The metaphor does not naturally encode the
  constraints of social structure, institutional rules, and power
  dynamics that shape what actions are available. A person acting within
  a bureaucracy is not moving through open terrain; they are moving
  through a maze they did not build.

- **The metaphor individualizes collective action** -- self-propulsion is
  a property of a single body. When groups act -- a union strikes, a
  community organizes, a team executes -- the self-propelled motion
  model either assigns agency to a single leader or vaguely distributes
  it across the group. Neither captures the emergent nature of collective
  action.

## Expressions

- "She moved on her own initiative" -- acting without external cause
- "He took the first step" -- initiating action as beginning locomotion
- "They set out to change things" -- purposeful action as beginning a
  self-propelled journey
- "She pushed herself forward" -- effortful action as strenuous
  self-propulsion
- "He pulled back from the project" -- ceasing action as reversing motion
- "Keep moving forward" -- sustained action as continued self-propulsion
- "She drove herself to succeed" -- intense effort as forceful
  self-propulsion
- "He got going on the report" -- beginning action as initiating motion
- "They forged ahead" -- determined action as powerful forward movement
- "She stepped up" -- accepting responsibility as moving oneself to a
  higher position

## Origin Story

ACTION IS SELF-PROPELLED MOTION is identified in the Master Metaphor List
(Lakoff, Espenson & Schwartz, 1991) and elaborated in *Philosophy in the
Flesh* (Lakoff & Johnson, 1999) as a core component of the Event Structure
metaphor system. Lakoff presents it as the agentive dual of CHANGES ARE
MOTIONS: changes can happen to you (caused by external forces), but actions
are what you do to the world through your own volition. The self-propelled
element is what distinguishes action from mere change -- the agent is both
the source and the subject of motion.

This mapping is closely related to but distinct from ACTIONS ARE
SELF-PROPELLED MOTIONS: the plural form documents the general linguistic
pattern, while this singular form names the metaphor's role within the
Event Structure system. Both reflect the same embodied grounding in
volitional bodily movement.
