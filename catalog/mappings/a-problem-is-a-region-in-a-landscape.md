## What It Brings

A problem is a place you are in -- a thicket, a swamp, a maze, a rough
patch of terrain. You entered it, you are stuck in it, and solving the
problem is finding your way out. This metaphor maps spatial navigation
through difficult terrain onto the experience of confronting and resolving
problems. Where A PROBLEM IS A LOCKED CONTAINER puts the solver outside
looking in, this metaphor puts the solver inside looking for an exit.

Key structural parallels:

- **Being in a problem is being in a difficult region** -- "I'm stuck in
  this mess." "We're in deep trouble." "She's mired in debt." The problem
  is not an object you confront but a territory you inhabit. You are
  surrounded by the problem, immersed in its conditions, and your
  movement is constrained by its features.

- **The problem's difficulty is the terrain's harshness** -- "It's a
  thorny issue." "We're in rough waters." "This is a minefield." Hard
  problems are mapped onto terrain that is hard to traverse: swamps that
  slow you, thickets that entangle, cliffs that block. The difficulty
  is not a lock to pick but ground to cross.

- **Solving is finding a way out** -- "I see a way out." "She found her
  way through." "We need to navigate this carefully." The solution is not
  an object to extract but a path to discover. Solving requires
  orientation, route-finding, and movement -- cognitive skills mapped
  onto navigational ones.

- **Being lost is being unable to solve** -- "I'm lost on this problem."
  "I don't know which way to turn." "I can't see my way through." When
  the solver cannot find a path, the experience is disorientation --
  the cognitive equivalent of not knowing where you are or which direction
  leads out.

- **Others can guide you out** -- "She pointed me in the right direction."
  "He helped me find my way." "They showed me the way out." Because the
  problem is a landscape, others who have been there before can serve as
  guides. Expertise is familiarity with the terrain.

## Where It Breaks

- **The metaphor assumes the solver is already inside** -- it has no good
  model for problems you can see coming but have not yet entered. Problems
  that are anticipated, planned for, or avoided entirely do not fit the
  "you are in a difficult region" structure. Prevention is not navigation.

- **Landscapes have fixed topology** -- the metaphor implies that the
  problem has a stable structure that can be mapped and navigated. But
  many problems shift as you engage with them: political crises evolve,
  interpersonal conflicts escalate, technical problems cascade. A problem
  that changes shape while you traverse it breaks the landscape metaphor.

- **The metaphor hides problem dissolution** -- sometimes the best
  resolution is not finding a way out but realizing you were never really
  in the region you thought. Reframing a problem, dissolving it by
  changing the question, or discovering it was a pseudo-problem -- none
  of these fit "navigating out of a region." You cannot navigate out of
  a place that turns out not to exist.

- **The binary of in/out oversimplifies** -- you are either in the
  problem-region or out of it. But many problems are partially resolved,
  managed rather than solved, or transformed into different (perhaps
  better) problems. The landscape metaphor wants a clean exit where
  often there is only gradual amelioration.

- **It makes problem avoidance look like cowardice** -- if a problem is
  a region, then not entering it can look like going around it, which
  inherits the connotation of avoiding difficulty. But strategic problem
  avoidance -- choosing not to engage with certain issues -- is often
  wisdom, not evasion.

## Expressions

- "I'm stuck in this problem" -- immobility in a difficult region
- "I see a way out" -- discovering a path through the terrain
- "We're in deep trouble" -- being in a region from which escape is hard
- "It's a thorny issue" -- a region with painful obstacles
- "Navigate the difficulties" -- finding a route through rough terrain
- "I'm lost on this" -- disorientation in the problem-landscape
- "She found her way through" -- successful traversal of a difficult
  region
- "We're in uncharted territory" -- a problem-region with no known paths
- "He's in over his head" -- the region is deeper than expected
- "There's no way out" -- the problem-region has no exit
- "We need to get out of this mess" -- escaping a disordered region

## Origin Story

This metaphor is documented in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson
& Schwartz, 1991) and connects to the broader Event Structure metaphor
system. It is a specific elaboration of STATES ARE LOCATIONS: if being in
a state is being in a location, then being in a problematic state is being
in a difficult location. The landscape element adds navigational structure
-- problems are not just locations but regions with topology, terrain
features, and paths through them.

The metaphor draws on the same embodied experience as the journey family:
humans are navigators who move through space and encounter difficult
terrain. The difference from LIFE IS A JOURNEY is specificity. The journey
metaphor covers the whole trajectory of a purposeful activity; the
landscape-problem metaphor zooms in on the difficult stretches where
forward motion is impeded and the path is unclear.
