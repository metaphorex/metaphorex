## What It Brings

Neal Stephenson coined "metaverse" in *Snow Crash* (1992) to describe a
shared, persistent virtual world that users experience through avatars --
a planet-sized virtual city accessed via goggles and earphones, where
real estate has value, social hierarchies form, and economic activity
occurs. The word migrated from science fiction into technology discourse
so thoroughly that by 2021, Facebook renamed itself Meta to claim the
concept as its corporate mission. Today "metaverse" is used without
reference to Stephenson by the vast majority of people who say it.

Key structural parallels:

- **The virtual as a place** -- Stephenson's most consequential import is
  spatial. The metaverse is not a service, a platform, or a protocol; it
  is a place you go to. This spatial framing shapes how designers build
  virtual environments (with landscapes, buildings, and distances), how
  users navigate them (by walking, flying, or teleporting), and how
  economies form within them (through real estate, location, and proximity).
  The metaphor makes "where are you?" a meaningful question about a
  software system.
- **Persistent shared reality** -- Stephenson's metaverse continues to
  exist when you log off. Other people are in it while you are not. This
  imports the structure of physical reality (the world does not pause when
  you close your eyes) onto a computational system, creating expectations
  of continuity, consequence, and shared experience that fundamentally
  differ from session-based software.
- **The avatar as self-presentation** -- in the metaverse, you exist as
  an avatar: a visual representation that may or may not resemble your
  physical body. The metaphor imports the entire sociology of identity
  performance -- how you present yourself, how others perceive you, the
  gap between representation and reality -- into a digital context. The
  word "avatar" itself is borrowed from Hindu theology (the incarnation
  of a deity), adding another metaphorical layer.
- **Economic migration** -- Stephenson's metaverse has a functioning
  economy with property, commerce, and class distinctions. The metaphor
  imports the assumption that virtual spaces will develop real economic
  weight -- that digital land can be scarce, digital goods can have value,
  and digital labor can produce real wealth. This framing shaped the NFT
  boom, virtual real estate speculation, and the entire "web3" narrative.

## Where It Breaks

- **The spatial metaphor constrains design** -- by insisting that virtual
  experience must be organized as a place, the metaverse metaphor excludes
  forms of digital interaction that are not spatial: text conversations,
  collaborative documents, social media feeds, recommendation streams.
  These are arguably more important forms of shared digital experience than
  walking around a virtual city, but the metaverse framing cannot see them
  because they do not look like places.
- **Stephenson's metaverse was dystopian** -- in *Snow Crash*, the
  metaverse exists because the physical world has collapsed into corporate
  franchises and privatized enclaves. People retreat into virtual reality
  because actual reality is unbearable. Technology companies that adopted
  the term stripped away this critical context, presenting the metaverse as
  aspirational rather than compensatory. The original metaphor carries a
  warning that its corporate inheritors systematically suppress.
- **Persistence requires infrastructure that nobody controls** -- a truly
  shared persistent virtual world requires interoperability across
  platforms, companies, and technologies. Stephenson could postulate this;
  engineers cannot build it. Every actual "metaverse" product is a walled
  garden controlled by a single company, which contradicts the core premise
  of a shared reality. The metaphor promises a commons and delivers a mall.
- **The avatar problem is unsolved** -- Stephenson's avatars are high-
  fidelity representations in a mature technology. Real avatar technology
  in the 2020s ranges from cartoonish to uncanny. The metaverse metaphor
  imports expectations of self-expression and social presence that the
  technology cannot deliver, creating a persistent gap between the concept
  and the experience.
- **Physical embodiment matters more than the metaphor admits** -- the
  metaverse frame treats the physical body as something to be transcended
  or replaced. But human cognition is embodied: we think with our hands,
  orient with our vestibular systems, and read social cues through
  micro-expressions. Virtual environments that ignore embodiment produce
  nausea, fatigue, and social disconnection -- problems that the spatial
  metaphor has no vocabulary to describe because it treats the body as
  optional.

## Expressions

- "The metaverse" -- any persistent shared virtual environment, used without
  awareness of its science fiction origin
- "Enter the metaverse" -- spatial language treating a software platform as
  a place to go
- "Build in the metaverse" -- the real estate metaphor, treating virtual
  space as developable land
- "Metaverse economy" -- economic activity within virtual worlds, from NFTs
  to virtual goods to digital labor
- "Avatar" -- the user's virtual self-representation, carrying the
  metaverse's identity-performance framework into any virtual context
- "Ready Player One" -- Ernest Cline's 2011 novel, which extended
  Stephenson's concept and became a secondary source for the metaverse
  metaphor in popular culture

## Origin Story

Stephenson wrote *Snow Crash* in 1992 as a satirical cyberpunk novel. The
metaverse was one element of a larger speculative world that also included
privatized national defense, Mafia-run pizza delivery, and a Sumerian
neurolinguistic virus. Technology readers extracted the metaverse concept
from this satirical context and treated it as a design specification. By
the late 1990s, virtual world platforms (ActiveWorlds, Second Life) were
explicitly building toward Stephenson's vision. The term entered mainstream
business discourse in 2021 when Mark Zuckerberg renamed Facebook to Meta
and declared the metaverse the company's future. This was the moment the
metaphor fully died: when a Fortune 10 company adopts a science fiction
term as its corporate identity, the fictional origin becomes invisible to
the general public. The subsequent collapse of Meta's metaverse ambitions
(tens of billions spent, modest adoption) suggests that the metaphor may
have been more compelling than the technology it described -- a common fate
for science fiction concepts that migrate into engineering, where narrative
coherence is no substitute for technical feasibility.
