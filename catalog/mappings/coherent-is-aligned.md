## What It Brings

When ideas "line up," when a plan "comes together," when the parts of a
theory "are in alignment" -- we understand abstract coherence through the
bodily experience of physical alignment. Things that point in the same
direction work together. Things that are crooked, skewed, or out of line
do not.

Key structural parallels:

- **Coherent elements as aligned objects** -- propositions, goals, or values
  that are coherent with each other are understood as physically parallel or
  pointing in the same direction. "Their goals are aligned" treats abstract
  compatibility as spatial orientation. Incoherence is deviation: a plan
  "goes off in too many directions."
- **Coherence as straightness** -- a coherent argument "follows a straight
  line." An incoherent one "wanders" or "zigzags." The metaphor privileges
  linear reasoning as the ideal and treats digression as a physical defect.
- **Integration as bringing-into-line** -- making something coherent is an
  act of straightening. You "straighten out" a confused account. You "bring
  things into line." The editorial act of making a text coherent maps onto
  the physical act of alignment.
- **Coherence as structural integrity** -- aligned elements bear load
  together; misaligned ones create stress points. A coherent strategy
  distributes effort effectively; an incoherent one works against itself.
  "We're pulling in different directions" is the failure mode.

## Where It Breaks

- **Coherence is not always linear** -- the metaphor privileges sequential,
  directional reasoning. But many forms of coherence are non-linear:
  ecological coherence, musical coherence, narrative coherence that loops
  back on itself. A jazz improvisation coheres without anything "lining up"
  in the geometric sense.
- **Productive incoherence exists** -- the metaphor treats misalignment as
  always bad. But creative breakthroughs often come from the collision of
  misaligned ideas. Brainstorming is deliberately incoherent. The metaphor
  has no vocabulary for the value of controlled divergence.
- **The metaphor assumes a reference direction** -- aligned with what?
  Physical alignment requires a reference axis. The metaphor smuggles in the
  assumption that there is a correct direction, which obscures the political
  question of who decides what counts as coherent. "Strategic alignment" in
  organizations often means alignment with leadership's priorities, but the
  metaphor makes this sound like a neutral geometric fact.
- **Flattens dimensionality** -- physical alignment operates in a small
  number of spatial dimensions. Intellectual coherence operates across many
  dimensions simultaneously (logical, ethical, aesthetic, practical). Two
  ideas can be aligned on one dimension and misaligned on another, but the
  metaphor pushes toward a single yes-or-no verdict.

## Expressions

- "Those ideas don't line up" -- logical inconsistency as spatial
  misalignment
- "Let me straighten that out" -- clarifying confusion as correcting a
  physical crookedness
- "We need to get aligned on this" -- organizational coherence framed as
  geometric operation
- "That argument goes off in too many directions" -- incoherence as
  divergent vectors
- "Her thinking is very straight" -- clarity as linearity
- "A crooked argument" -- dishonesty or incoherence as physical deviation
- "Pulling in different directions" -- organizational incoherence as
  opposed forces
- "Everything fell into place" -- coherence as objects reaching their
  correct spatial positions

## Origin Story

Listed in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson & Schwartz 1991) as
part of the broader pattern linking embodied spatial experience to abstract
cognitive evaluation. The metaphor is a specific instance of the more
general mapping from physical orientation to conceptual order. Its
contemporary prominence in corporate language ("strategic alignment,"
"aligned incentives") reflects how deeply the embodied intuition -- things
that point the same way work together -- structures organizational
thinking.
