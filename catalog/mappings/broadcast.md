## What It Brings

Scattering seeds by hand across a field -- a broad cast. The agricultural
technique is one of the oldest in human civilization: reach into a bag,
grab a handful of seed, and fling it widely. You do not aim each seed at a
specific spot. You trust that enough will land in fertile ground to produce
a harvest.

- **Undirected distribution** -- the defining structural property of
  broadcast sowing is that you do not choose where each seed lands. The
  mapping to radio (and later television) preserves this exactly: a
  transmitter sends a signal in all directions, and the broadcaster does
  not choose which receivers pick it up. This undirectedness is so central
  to the concept that "narrowcast" was coined specifically to describe its
  opposite -- a word that only makes sense if you know that "broadcast"
  means wide, uncontrolled scattering.
- **Redundancy as strategy** -- a farmer broadcasting seed throws far more
  than will germinate. Many seeds fall on stone, are eaten by birds, or
  land too close together. The farmer accepts waste as the cost of
  coverage. Radio broadcasting imports this same logic: most of the signal
  energy is wasted on empty fields, roads, and buildings. The broadcaster
  accepts the waste because reaching every possible receiver in the area
  is more important than efficiency.
- **One-to-many asymmetry** -- one sower, many landing points. One
  transmitter, many receivers. The metaphor encodes a fundamental
  communication architecture: a single source distributing content to an
  undifferentiated mass audience. This framing shaped 20th-century media
  theory (the "mass" in mass media is the scattered seed's landing field)
  and persists in network protocols where "broadcast" means sending a
  packet to all nodes on a segment.
- **No feedback loop** -- the sower does not know which seeds took root
  until harvest. The broadcaster does not know who is listening until
  ratings arrive. The metaphor imports a temporal gap between distribution
  and response that shaped how broadcast media operated for decades:
  produce content, scatter it, and wait to learn what happened.

## Where It Breaks

- **Seeds grow; signals do not** -- the agricultural metaphor implies that
  what is scattered will take root, develop, and produce something new.
  A radio signal does not grow. It arrives, is consumed, and is gone. The
  organic development built into the source domain -- germination, growth,
  harvest -- has no parallel in electronic communication. The metaphor
  borrows the scattering but discards the cultivation, which strips out
  the most interesting part of the agricultural process.
- **Modern "broadcasting" is increasingly targeted** -- digital platforms
  that call themselves broadcasters (YouTube, Spotify, podcasts) are not
  broadcasting in any structural sense. Algorithms select specific content
  for specific users. The metaphor persists as a category label while the
  underlying architecture has shifted from scatter to aim. When Spotify
  says it "broadcasts" a podcast, it is performing the opposite of a broad
  cast: it is narrowcasting through recommendation algorithms while using
  the vocabulary of seed-scattering.
- **The agricultural skill is lost** -- broadcast sowing required genuine
  expertise: the right wrist flick, the right walking pace, the right
  density for the crop. It was a physical skill passed between
  generations. None of this craft maps onto pressing "transmit." The
  metaphor elevated a technical act (sending radio signals) by associating
  it with an agricultural art, but the art's embodied knowledge was
  discarded in the borrowing.
- **Broadcast implies ground-level distribution** -- a sower walks across
  a field, distributing seed at ground level. Radio transmission works
  from elevated towers, sending signals above and outward. The spatial
  geometry is inverted: agricultural broadcasting is horizontal and
  proximate; electronic broadcasting is vertical and distant. This
  inversion is invisible because the metaphor died before anyone noticed
  the mismatch.

## Expressions

- "Broadcasting" -- transmitting content to a general audience, the
  standard usage that carries no agricultural residue
- "Broadcast network" -- a television or radio network structured around
  one-to-many distribution
- "Broadcast address" -- in networking, an address that sends a packet
  to all devices on a segment, the most technically precise survival of
  the scattering metaphor
- "Don't broadcast it" -- share something discreetly rather than widely,
  using the metaphor's scattering property as a warning
- "Narrowcast" -- target content to a specific audience, a word that only
  exists because "broadcast" established the scattering default
- "Broadcast journalism" -- news reporting for mass distribution, where
  "broadcast" functions as a medium label with no metaphorical content

## Origin Story

"Broadcast" as an agricultural term dates to at least the 18th century.
The "broad cast" was the wide, arcing throw of the sower's arm, as
opposed to "drilling" (planting seeds in narrow rows with a seed drill).
Jethro Tull's invention of the seed drill (1701) began the process of
making broadcast sowing obsolete in commercial agriculture, though the
technique persisted on small farms into the 20th century.

The metaphorical transfer to radio occurred in the early 1920s, during
the first years of commercial radio. The Pittsburgh station KDKA, often
credited as the first commercial broadcaster (1920), used the term to
describe its signal distribution. The agricultural metaphor was apt: early
radio transmitters scattered their signals like seed, reaching whoever
happened to have a receiver within range. The Pittsburgh Press used
"broadcasting" in its coverage of KDKA's 1920 election night broadcast,
and the term stuck.

The metaphor died quickly. By the 1930s, "broadcast" was simply what
radio stations did. By the 1950s, television inherited the term. By the
1990s, the internet began eroding the one-to-many architecture that made
the metaphor structurally valid, but the word persisted. Today,
"broadcast" is used for podcasts (which are pulled, not pushed), social
media posts (which are algorithmically filtered, not scattered), and
email blasts (which are addressed, not undirected). The word has
outlived every structural property it originally encoded.

  United States to 1933* (1966) -- definitive history of early radio
