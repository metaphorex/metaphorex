## What It Brings

A schedule moves toward you. Deadlines approach, dates come closer, and
events arrive. In this metaphor, a schedule is not a static plan that you
move through but a moving entity that advances under its own momentum. The
person stands still while temporal commitments come at them -- an instance
of the TIME IS A MOVING OBJECT variant of time metaphors, specialized to
the domain of planned and structured time.

Key structural parallels:

- **Scheduled events approach the observer** -- "The deadline is coming
  up." "The meeting is approaching." "The launch date is almost here."
  Events on a schedule are conceptualized as objects moving through space
  toward a stationary observer. The nearer the event, the sooner it will
  happen. Temporal proximity is spatial proximity.

- **Schedules have velocity** -- "The project timeline is accelerating."
  "Things are moving faster than expected." "The deadline is racing toward
  us." A schedule can move quickly or slowly, and its speed maps onto how
  rapidly scheduled events are arriving. A schedule that moves fast creates
  urgency; one that moves slowly gives breathing room.

- **Schedules can be delayed or advanced** -- "We pushed the deadline
  back." "The meeting was moved up." "The event was held back." Because
  the schedule is a moving object, it can be acted on with force: pushed
  away (delayed) or pulled closer (advanced). This gives schedule
  manipulation a physical, force-dynamic character.

- **Schedule collisions** -- "We have a conflict on Tuesday." "Two
  deadlines are hitting at the same time." "Everything is piling up."
  When multiple scheduled events arrive simultaneously, they collide.
  The metaphor maps physical collision onto temporal overlap, producing
  the experience of being overwhelmed by converging commitments.

- **The schedule as an oncoming force** -- "I'm facing a wall of
  deadlines." "The schedule is bearing down on us." "It's coming whether
  we're ready or not." The moving schedule is not always friendly.
  When the observer is unprepared, the approaching schedule feels
  threatening -- an object on a collision course with the observer.

## Where It Breaks

- **The metaphor makes the person passive** -- the schedule moves; the
  person stands still and receives it. But in reality, people actively
  construct, negotiate, and reshape their schedules. The moving-object
  model has no natural vocabulary for schedule-making as a creative act.
  It frames the person as a target of temporal events rather than their
  author.

- **Schedules are not single objects** -- a schedule is a complex
  structure of interdependent commitments, priorities, and constraints.
  The moving-object metaphor flattens this into a thing that approaches,
  losing the internal structure. You cannot "dodge" one part of a
  schedule while accepting another the way you might sidestep a physical
  object.

- **The metaphor obscures agency over time** -- by making the schedule
  the mover, the metaphor hides the human decisions that created the
  schedule in the first place. "The deadline is approaching" sounds like
  a natural event, but someone set that deadline. The metaphor naturalizes
  what is actually a social construction.

- **Speed is not uniform** -- the metaphor implies that the schedule moves
  at a constant rate (matching clock time), but the subjective experience
  of time under a schedule is highly variable. The week before a deadline
  feels faster than the week after. The moving-object model cannot
  account for this subjective acceleration without awkwardly making the
  object speed up.

- **It cannot model schedule flexibility** -- agile, adaptive scheduling
  where commitments are renegotiated continuously does not fit an object
  on a fixed trajectory. The metaphor works best for rigid, externally
  imposed schedules and poorly for collaborative, evolving ones.

## Expressions

- "The deadline is approaching" -- a scheduled event moving toward the
  observer
- "The meeting is coming up" -- temporal proximity as spatial approach
- "We pushed the deadline back" -- delaying by applying force to the
  moving object
- "The launch date was moved up" -- advancing by pulling the object closer
- "Everything is piling up" -- multiple scheduled events arriving and
  accumulating
- "The schedule is bearing down on us" -- the schedule as a threatening
  approaching force
- "The due date crept up on me" -- the schedule arriving without the
  observer noticing its approach
- "We have a conflict next week" -- two scheduled objects colliding
- "The timeline is slipping" -- the schedule moving in the wrong direction
- "It came sooner than expected" -- the moving object arriving early

## Origin Story

This metaphor is documented in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson
& Schwartz, 1991) and is a specific instance of the more general TIME IS
A MOVING OBJECT mapping identified by Lakoff and Johnson. In *Metaphors
We Live By* (1980), they describe two complementary time metaphors: in
one, the observer moves through time (TIME IS A LANDSCAPE WE MOVE
THROUGH); in the other, time moves past the observer (TIME IS A MOVING
OBJECT). A SCHEDULE IS A MOVING OBJECT applies the second variant
specifically to structured, planned time -- the kind of time that
dominates modern life.

The metaphor is particularly productive in work contexts, where schedules,
deadlines, and timelines are the primary way people experience temporal
pressure. The moving-object variant dominates schedule talk because
schedules are typically experienced as externally imposed -- they come at
you whether you are ready or not.
