## What It Brings

King Midas of Phrygia asked Dionysus for the power to turn everything
he touched into gold. The god granted the wish. Midas rejoiced as he
turned objects to gold -- until he touched his food (which became
inedible metal), his drink (which became liquid gold in his throat),
and in some versions his daughter (who became a golden statue). The
gift became a curse. The metaphor maps this structure -- the ability
to make everything profitable or successful, and the dangers of that
ability -- onto economics, business, and any domain where optimization
toward a single metric destroys everything else.

- **The positive reading: effortless success** -- in its most common
  modern usage, "the Midas touch" simply means the ability to make
  money or succeed at whatever you attempt. The myth's cautionary
  ending is dropped entirely. "She has the Midas touch" is a
  compliment, not a warning. The metaphor imports only the golden
  transformation -- the structure of a person whose involvement
  reliably converts ordinary ventures into valuable ones. This maps
  onto serial entrepreneurs, investors with consistent returns, and
  executives who turn around every division they manage.
- **The negative reading: monomaniacal optimization** -- in its
  fuller mythological reading, the Midas touch warns that optimizing
  everything toward a single value (gold, profit, one metric)
  destroys the diversity of values that makes life livable. Food
  must be food, not gold. A daughter must be a daughter, not a
  statue. The metaphor maps this structure onto Goodhart's Law
  (when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure),
  metric fixation in organizations, and any system where maximizing
  one variable destroys the other variables that the system depends
  on. A company that optimizes purely for quarterly revenue may
  destroy employee morale, product quality, and customer trust --
  turning everything to gold and finding that gold is inedible.
- **The transformation is indiscriminate** -- Midas cannot choose
  what to turn to gold. The power applies to everything he touches,
  without exception or control. The metaphor imports the structure
  of an optimization function that has no off switch: it converts
  all inputs, including the ones you need to remain unconverted.
  This maps onto algorithmic systems that optimize engagement at
  the cost of mental health, cost-cutting programs that eliminate
  essential functions along with waste, and standardization efforts
  that destroy valuable local variation.
- **The wish was granted, not imposed** -- Midas asked for this
  power. He chose it. The metaphor imports the structure of a
  self-inflicted curse: the dangerous optimization was not forced
  upon the system by external circumstances but was actively
  requested by the decision-maker who later suffers from it. This
  maps onto executives who demanded the KPI regime that now
  distorts their organization, founders who insisted on growth
  metrics that now prevent sustainable operation, and any leader
  who got exactly what they asked for and discovered it was
  destructive.

## Where It Breaks

- **The two readings contradict each other** -- "the Midas touch"
  is simultaneously a compliment (you succeed at everything) and
  a warning (single-metric optimization destroys value). In
  practice, speakers almost always mean the compliment. This
  creates a dead metaphor that has inverted its source: the myth
  is a cautionary tale about the dangers of gold-obsession, but
  the modern expression celebrates exactly the quality the myth
  warns against. Using "the Midas touch" as praise requires
  suppressing the entire moral of the story.
- **Midas's problem was literal, not structural** -- in the myth,
  the golden touch is a physical transformation. Real "Midas touch"
  problems are structural: metric fixation, incentive misalignment,
  organizational monoculture. The gap between a literal magical
  curse and a systemic organizational dysfunction is large enough
  that the metaphor's explanatory power is limited. Saying a
  company "has the Midas touch problem" sounds vivid but does
  not actually explain the mechanism by which over-optimization
  occurs or suggest how to fix it.
- **Midas could reverse the curse** -- Dionysus told Midas to wash
  in the river Pactolus, and the golden touch was removed. The myth
  has a happy ending (in most versions). But the metaphor, when
  used as a warning, implies irreversibility -- once you have
  optimized everything toward one metric, you cannot undo the
  damage. The mythological source actually contradicts the
  metaphor's implied fatalism: Midas got a second chance. Real
  organizations sometimes do too (pivots, restructuring, new
  leadership), but the metaphor does not acknowledge this
  possibility.
- **The metaphor personalizes a systemic problem** -- "the Midas
  touch" attaches to an individual: the CEO, the investor, the
  entrepreneur. But metric fixation in organizations is rarely
  the product of one person's wishes. It emerges from incentive
  structures, market pressures, board expectations, and cultural
  norms. Framing over-optimization as one person's Midas touch
  obscures the systemic factors and implies that replacing the
  individual would solve the problem.
- **Gold is actually valuable** -- in the myth, gold is desirable
  but insufficient. In many real-world Midas scenarios, the
  single metric being optimized is not even genuinely valuable --
  it is a proxy metric (click-through rates, lines of code, paper
  citations) that has been mistaken for the thing it measures.
  Midas at least wanted something real. Many organizational Midas
  problems involve optimizing for something that was never gold
  to begin with.

## Expressions

- "The Midas touch" -- the standard idiom for the ability to make
  everything profitable or successful, almost always used as a
  compliment despite the myth's cautionary meaning
- "Everything he touches turns to gold" -- the expanded form,
  describing consistent success across domains, fully detached
  from the mythological warning
- "A Midas problem" -- the rarer, myth-aware usage describing
  the danger of single-metric optimization, where converting
  everything to one type of value destroys necessary diversity
- "Golden touch" -- the further-generalized variant that has
  fully dropped the mythological name, used as a pure synonym
  for consistent business success
- "Turning to gold" -- describing the process of making something
  profitable or valuable, used in business contexts without
  awareness of the Midas source

## Origin Story

The Midas myth is recorded in several ancient sources. Ovid's
*Metamorphoses* (8 CE), Book 11, provides the most influential
version: Midas hosts Silenus (a companion of Dionysus), and in
gratitude Dionysus offers Midas any wish. Midas asks for the
golden touch, regrets it when he cannot eat or drink, and is
freed by washing in the river Pactolus. Aristotle references
the Midas story in the *Politics* (c. 350 BCE) as an illustration
of the difference between wealth and well-being -- one of the
earliest uses of Midas as a metaphor for economic critique.

The historical Midas was likely a real king of Phrygia (c. 740-696
BCE). Archaeological evidence from Gordion, the Phrygian capital,
confirms a wealthy kingdom, and the river Pactolus near Sardis was
indeed a source of alluvial gold in antiquity -- the material basis
for the legend.

"The Midas touch" entered English as a metaphorical expression by
the 16th century. Its transformation from warning to compliment
occurred gradually over the 19th and 20th centuries, as capitalist
culture revalued the ability to generate wealth from a vice (greed)
to a virtue (entrepreneurship). By the mid-20th century, "the
Midas touch" was overwhelmingly positive in popular usage, and the
myth's cautionary dimension survived mainly in literary and
academic contexts.

## References

- Ovid. *Metamorphoses*, Book 11 (8 CE) -- the most influential
  ancient version of the Midas story, including the golden touch
  and its reversal
- Aristotle. *Politics*, Book 1.9 (c. 350 BCE) -- uses Midas as
  an example of the distinction between wealth-as-means and
  wealth-as-end
- Hawthorne, Nathaniel. "The Golden Touch" in *A Wonder-Book for
  Girls and Boys* (1851) -- the Victorian retelling that added
  the daughter-turned-to-gold, now the most widely known version
  of the myth
- Muller, Jerry Z. *The Tyranny of Metrics* (2018) -- a modern
  analysis of metric fixation in institutions that maps directly
  onto the Midas structure, though without using the metaphor
  explicitly
