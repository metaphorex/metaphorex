## What It Brings

A small creature that fits in your hand, scurries across a surface, and has
a tail. The mapping is almost entirely morphological -- shape, size, and the
trailing cable -- rather than behavioral. This makes the mouse unusual among
computing metaphors: most map functional structure (virus, bug, firewall),
but the mouse maps physical appearance. It is a metaphor of resemblance,
not of process.

- **Intuitive scale and domesticity** -- mice are small, familiar, harmless.
  The name made a novel input device feel approachable at a time when
  computers were intimidating room-sized machines operated by specialists.
  You hold a mouse in your palm. You control it with small, natural hand
  movements. The animal metaphor reinforced the device's design intent:
  computing should be as natural as reaching for a small creature on your
  desk.
- **The tail** -- the original mouse had a cable trailing from its rear,
  completing the visual resemblance. The cable-as-tail was the most
  legible part of the metaphor, the feature that made the name feel
  inevitable rather than arbitrary. When Engelbart's colleagues saw the
  device, the name required no explanation.
- **The CAT connection** -- in Engelbart's lab, the on-screen cursor was
  tracked by a program called CAT. The mouse was the physical device that
  the CAT seemed to chase across the screen. This predator-prey dynamic
  was a playful secondary mapping that most users never knew about, but it
  reveals how deeply the animal metaphor ran in the original design context.

## Where It Breaks

- **The physical basis disappeared** -- wireless mice have no tail. Optical
  mice have no ball (another lost morphological feature -- the original
  mouse rolled on a rubber ball, like a creature running). Trackpads,
  touchscreens, and gesture interfaces have no physical mouse at all. The
  name persists for a device that no longer resembles its namesake in any
  way. This is the purest possible case of a dead metaphor: the source
  referent has been physically removed, and nobody notices.
- **No behavioral mapping survived** -- unlike "bug" (which imports hiding
  and hunting behavior) or "virus" (which imports replication and
  contagion), the mouse imports nothing from animal behavior. Mice scurry,
  hide, gnaw, and breed. None of this maps to the input device. The
  metaphor was always and only about shape. When the shape changed, the
  metaphor had nothing left -- yet the word stayed.
- **Plural confusion as a tombstone** -- the uncertain plural ("mouses"
  vs. "mice" for the device) is a linguistic fossil. If the metaphor were
  alive, the plural would obviously be "mice." The fact that "mouses"
  exists as an alternative signals that speakers no longer connect the
  device to the animal strongly enough for the animal's morphology to
  govern the word's grammar.
- **The name blocks conceptual evolution** -- calling a trackpad or
  gesture interface a "mouse" (as some users do colloquially) forces a
  physical-device mental model onto interactions that are fundamentally
  different. The dead metaphor anchors thinking to a hardware form factor
  that is disappearing, making it harder to conceptualize post-mouse
  interaction paradigms.

## Expressions

- "Mouse click" -- pressing the button on the device, now used for any
  selection action regardless of input device
- "Mouse over" -- hovering the cursor, an interaction concept named for
  hardware that many users no longer physically use
- "Mousepad" -- the surface the mouse runs on, a habitat for the creature,
  now a vestigial accessory for optical mice that work on any surface
- "Mouse pointer" -- the on-screen cursor, still called this even on
  touchscreen devices that have no mouse
- "Right-click" -- a mouse-specific interaction that has become a generic
  term for context menus, regardless of how they are invoked
- "Drag and drop" -- an interaction pattern designed around the mouse's
  physical affordances, now applied to touch and gesture interfaces

## Origin Story

Douglas Engelbart and Bill English built the first mouse at the Stanford
Research Institute in 1964 and demonstrated it publicly on December 9,
1968, in what is now called "The Mother of All Demos." The device was a
wooden shell with a single button, rolling on two perpendicular wheels
(later replaced by a ball), with a cable emerging from the rear.

Engelbart's published account states that the team called it a "mouse"
because of the tail-like cable. The companion story -- that the on-screen
cursor-tracking program was called CAT, making the mouse something chased
by a cat -- appears in several accounts from SRI colleagues but is not
confirmed in Engelbart's own writings. Whether or not the CAT story is
apocryphal, it reveals how naturally the animal metaphor extended in the
minds of the device's creators.

The mouse remained a niche research tool until Xerox PARC incorporated it
into the Alto (1973) and Apple popularized it with the Macintosh (1984).
By the 1990s, every personal computer shipped with a mouse, and the
metaphor was completely dead. The transition to wireless mice in the 2000s
severed the last physical connection to the animal -- the tail -- without
prompting anyone to rename the device. The word had become the thing.
