## What It Brings

When we evaluate options in any domain -- careers, partners, ideas,
policies -- we often frame the activity as shopping. We "shop around" for
a mortgage, a doctor, a college. We "browse" options. We check the "price"
of a compromise. The commercial transaction provides the cognitive
scaffolding for how we understand deliberate comparison and selection.

Key structural parallels:

- **Options as merchandise** -- the things being evaluated are laid out
  like goods on a shelf. You examine them, compare features, weigh
  trade-offs. "What's out there?" treats available options as inventory.
  The metaphor turns abstract possibilities into concrete objects that can
  be inspected and handled.
- **The seeker as shopper** -- the person evaluating options is a consumer
  moving through a marketplace. You "shop around" for health insurance.
  You "browse" job listings. The metaphor assigns the evaluator a familiar
  role with clear scripts: enter, browse, compare, select, leave.
- **Selection as purchase** -- choosing is buying. You "buy into" a plan.
  You "invest in" a relationship. The metaphor imports the transactional
  frame: choice is an exchange where you give up something (time,
  opportunity cost) to acquire something else.
- **Quality as value** -- the merits of an option are understood as
  market value. A "good deal" is a favorable choice. Getting "more for
  your money" applies even when no money changes hands. The metaphor
  provides a built-in evaluation metric borrowed from price.

## Where It Breaks

- **Not all evaluation is transactional** -- the shopping metaphor assumes
  that options can be compared on a common metric (price). But many of the
  most important choices resist commensuration. Choosing between two career
  paths, two ethical commitments, or two friendships cannot be reduced to
  "which is the better deal." The metaphor flattens incommensurable values
  into a single axis of comparison.
- **The shopper is sovereign; the chooser often is not** -- shopping implies
  autonomy. The shopper walks in and out freely. But many real decisions are
  constrained: you cannot simply "shop around" for a new country, a new
  family, or a new body. The metaphor imports a freedom of choice that may
  not exist.
- **Shopping has a clear endpoint; seeking may not** -- you leave the store
  with a purchase. But comparing and seeking can be open-ended.
  "Shopping around" for an answer to an existential question has no
  checkout counter. The metaphor imposes closure on processes that may
  be genuinely unresolvable.
- **The marketplace metaphor commodifies** -- framing options as merchandise
  transforms qualitative differences into quantitative ones. "Shopping for
  a college" subtly reduces education to a consumer product. "Shopping for
  a therapist" turns the therapeutic relationship into a retail transaction.
  The metaphor is efficient but corrosive to domains that resist
  commodification.

## Expressions

- "Shop around" -- evaluate multiple options before committing, applied to
  insurance, doctors, contractors, ideas
- "Window shopping" -- browsing options with no intent to commit
- "Browsing" -- casual evaluation, borrowed from retail to describe
  looking at job listings, dating profiles, or real estate
- "What's on the market?" -- treating available options as retail inventory
- "Bargain hunting" -- seeking the best option at the lowest cost, applied
  to everything from flights to legal services
- "Comparison shopping" -- systematic side-by-side evaluation, now the
  default mode for online consumer behavior
- "Buy into" -- accepting an idea or plan, framed as a purchase
- "I'm not buying it" -- rejecting a claim, treating belief as a
  transaction

## Origin Story

Listed in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson & Schwartz 1991) as
a mapping from the commercial domain onto general evaluation and
deliberation. The metaphor reflects the centrality of market behavior in
English-speaking cultures: the marketplace is one of the most familiar
structured environments for comparing and selecting, and its scripts
transfer readily to non-commercial contexts. The metaphor has intensified
in the internet era, where "browsing" and "shopping around" have become
the default verbs for any kind of online exploration.
