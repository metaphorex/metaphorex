## What It Brings

Double etymology, double metaphor. "Trolling" in internet slang draws from
two unrelated sources: the Scandinavian folklore creature that lurks under
bridges, and the fishing technique of dragging baited hooks through water.
The fishing sense came first in online usage -- Usenet users in the early
1990s spoke of "trolling for newbies," meaning dragging provocative bait
through discussion groups to see who would bite. But the folklore creature
eventually dominated the popular imagination, and today most people picture
the monster, not the fishing rod.

- **Baiting as technique** -- the fishing metaphor maps precisely. The
  troll drags bait (a provocative statement) through a forum (the water)
  and waits for someone to bite (respond with outrage). The fisher is not
  interested in the fish for its own sake; the point is the act of
  catching. Similarly, an internet troll is not interested in the argument;
  the point is the reaction. The technique requires patience, knowledge
  of the environment, and the right lure.
- **Lurking presence at chokepoints** -- the folklore creature maps
  differently but equally well. The troll lives under the bridge, a place
  everyone must cross. Internet trolls inhabit the spaces where discourse
  must pass: comment sections, reply threads, community forums. They do
  not seek you out; they wait where you must go. The bridge-troll demands
  a toll for passage; the internet troll extracts attention as its toll.
- **Ugliness and antisociality** -- Norse trolls were ugly, solitary,
  hostile to humans, and lived in dark places (caves, under bridges).
  The mapping onto internet behavior is unflattering but resonant:
  trolling is widely understood as antisocial, ugly behavior performed
  from dark corners (anonymous accounts, throwaway profiles). The
  mythology imports moral judgment that the fishing metaphor alone
  would not carry.
- **Transformation in sunlight** -- in some Norse traditions, trolls turn
  to stone when exposed to sunlight. This maps onto the common
  observation that trolls lose their power when exposed -- when
  anonymity is stripped, when the behavior is named and made visible,
  the troll is neutralized.

## Where It Breaks

- **Trolls were physical threats; internet trolls are rhetorical ones** --
  the folklore troll could eat you. An internet troll can waste your time
  and upset you, but the metaphor dramatically inflates the danger. This
  inflation cuts both ways: it makes trolling sound more threatening than
  it often is (someone posting bad-faith arguments), while simultaneously
  trivializing genuine online harassment by filing it under the same
  cartoon-monster category.
- **The fishing metaphor requires skill; much trolling is artless** --
  trolling as a fishing technique is skilled work. You need to know the
  water, choose the right bait, and maintain the right speed. Early
  Usenet trolling was similarly skilled: well-crafted provocations
  designed to be indistinguishable from sincere posts. Modern "trolling"
  often means crude insults and harassment, which requires no skill at
  all. The metaphor's original connotation of craft has been lost.
- **The mythology treats trolls as a natural species; online trolling is
  a choice** -- calling someone "a troll" rather than saying "they are
  trolling" essentializes the behavior. It suggests a fixed identity
  rather than a chosen action. This matters because it implies that some
  people are simply trolls by nature, which forecloses the possibility
  of accountability or change.
- **The bridge-troll metaphor hides systemic incentives** -- folklore
  trolls are individually motivated monsters. But large-scale online
  trolling is often systematically incentivized: platforms reward
  engagement, algorithms amplify outrage, and state actors deploy troll
  farms strategically. The folklore frame individualizes what is often
  an institutional or structural phenomenon.
- **"Don't feed the troll" misapplies the folklore** -- the standard
  advice imports the idea that trolls need sustenance (attention) and
  will starve without it. But unlike folklore creatures, real harassers
  do not go away when ignored. The advice, derived from the metaphor's
  internal logic, can be actively harmful when applied to sustained
  harassment campaigns.

## Expressions

- "Don't feed the troll" -- the canonical advice, treating attention as
  food that sustains the creature
- "Trolling for responses" -- the fishing usage, still visible in the
  gerund form
- "He's a troll" -- the essentializing move, converting a behavior into
  an identity
- "Troll farm" -- organized trolling operations, usually state-sponsored,
  extending the metaphor from individual monster to industrial agriculture
- "Troll post" -- a deliberately provocative message, the bait on the hook
- "Under the bridge" -- where trolls live, metaphorically applied to
  lurking in comment sections and forums

## Origin Story

The fishing verb "to troll" (dragging a baited hook through water) entered
English from Old French *troller* (to wander, to hunt) in the 14th
century. It has no etymological connection to the Norse *troll* (a
supernatural creature), though the phonetic coincidence proved irresistible.

Internet trolling originated on Usenet in the late 1980s and early 1990s.
The earliest documented uses describe "trolling for newbies" -- posting
deliberately naive questions in newsgroups to identify and mock
inexperienced users who took the bait. The term appeared in the alt.folklore.urban
FAQ as early as 1992. At this stage, the fishing metaphor was primary:
trolling was a technique, not an identity.

The shift from verb (trolling) to noun (troll) happened through the 1990s,
and with it the folklore creature displaced the fishing technique as the
dominant image. By the time trolling entered mainstream media discourse in
the 2000s, journalists invariably illustrated stories with images of
bridge-dwelling monsters, not fishing boats. The Norse etymology won the
popular imagination even though the fishing etymology was historically
correct.

The term further transformed in the 2010s when "troll" expanded from
describing individual provocateurs to describing state-sponsored
disinformation operations. Russia's Internet Research Agency, exposed in
2014-2016, was universally called a "troll farm" -- a compound that yokes
the folklore creature to industrial agriculture, two metaphors from
entirely different domains compressed into a single term.
