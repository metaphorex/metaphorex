## What It Brings

What comes first causes what comes after. This metaphor maps the structure
of temporal sequence onto the structure of causal explanation, so that
being earlier in time becomes being higher in the causal hierarchy. The
mapping is so deeply embedded in ordinary reasoning that it takes
philosophical effort to see it as a metaphor at all -- the Latin word
"causa" itself originally meant "that which falls before."

Key structural parallels:

- **Temporal order as causal order** -- "The argument started because he
  arrived late." "First the rain, then the flood." "What led up to the
  crisis?" In narrative explanation, causes are presented before effects,
  and this temporal ordering is taken as evidence of causal connection.
  The metaphor equates the sequence of events with the structure of
  explanation.
- **Priority as causal power** -- "The root cause," "the underlying
  problem," "what came first." That which is temporally prior is causally
  fundamental. The metaphor creates a hierarchy: earlier events are more
  causally important than later ones, origins matter more than
  developments, and the "first mover" has special explanatory privilege.
- **The causal chain as timeline** -- "a chain of events," "one thing
  led to another," "the sequence of causes." Causal structure is
  represented as a temporal sequence, a line of events stretching from
  past to present. Each link in the chain is both an effect of what
  came before and a cause of what comes after.
- **Explanatory regression as temporal regression** -- "We need to go
  back further." "What was the original cause?" "Trace it back to the
  beginning." To explain something more deeply is to go further back
  in time. The metaphor makes explanatory depth equivalent to temporal
  distance from the present.

## Where It Breaks

- **Temporal precedence is not causation** -- this is the classic *post
  hoc ergo propter hoc* fallacy, and the metaphor is what makes it feel
  intuitive. The rooster crows before sunrise, but the rooster does not
  cause the sun to rise. The metaphor's deep embedding in ordinary
  reasoning makes this error feel natural rather than fallacious.
- **Simultaneous causation exists** -- in physics, gravitational
  attraction is simultaneous: the Earth and the Moon pull on each
  other at the same instant. In economics, supply and demand determine
  price simultaneously. The temporal-precedence metaphor cannot
  represent causation that occurs without a time gap between cause
  and effect.
- **Causal importance is not temporal priority** -- the metaphor says
  that what comes first is most causally important. But a long-standing
  background condition (the presence of oxygen) may be temporally prior
  to an event (striking a match) without being the most causally
  relevant factor. The metaphor privileges origins over proximate
  causes in ways that can distort explanation.
- **Feedback loops break the chain** -- the metaphor structures
  causation as a one-directional timeline. But many causal systems
  involve feedback: A causes B, which causes A to intensify, which
  causes B to intensify. The temporal-precedence metaphor cannot
  represent circular causation without breaking the chain into an
  infinite regress.
- **Backward causation is conceptually possible** -- some
  interpretations of quantum mechanics and some philosophical thought
  experiments involve effects preceding their causes. The
  temporal-precedence metaphor rules this out by definition, which
  may be a feature (it reflects our actual causal intuitions) or a
  bug (it forecloses legitimate theoretical possibilities).

## Expressions

- "One thing led to another" -- temporal sequence as causal chain
- "What came first, the chicken or the egg?" -- causal priority as
  temporal priority
- "Trace it back to the root cause" -- deeper explanation as earlier
  origin
- "That preceded the crisis" -- temporal priority implying causal
  responsibility
- "In the wake of the disaster" -- effects as what follows temporally
- "The events leading up to the war" -- causes as temporally prior
  events
- "It all started when..." -- causal explanation as narrative beginning
- "First A, then B, therefore A caused B" -- the post hoc fallacy
  made explicit
- "What set this in motion?" -- the first cause as the temporal
  initiator

## Origin Story

CAUSAL PRECEDENCE IS TEMPORAL PRECEDENCE is documented in the Master
Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson & Schwartz, 1991) as part of the
causation cluster. The mapping is ancient: Aristotle's analysis of
the four causes already grappled with the relationship between temporal
and causal priority, and Hume's famous analysis of causation (1739)
explicitly identified "priority in time" as one of the three criteria
for causal connection (alongside contiguity and constant conjunction).

The metaphor's power lies in its near-invisibility. Narrative structure
in every human language places causes before effects, and this convention
is so basic that it feels like a feature of reality rather than a feature
of our representational system. Lakoff and Johnson's contribution was to
identify this as a metaphorical mapping rather than a transparent
description of how causation actually works.
