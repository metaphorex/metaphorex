## What It Brings

Old English *stocc* meant tree trunk -- a solid piece of wood. From this
single material origin, an entire financial vocabulary grew. The critical
intermediate step was the tally stick: a piece of wood split lengthwise to
record a debt. The creditor kept the larger half, called "the stock." The
debtor kept the smaller half, called "the stub." When the two halves were
matched, the debt was verified.

- **Material evidence of obligation** -- the tally stick was not a symbol
  of a debt; it *was* the debt, in the same way that a broken coin shared
  between lovers is not a symbol of the relationship but its physical
  instantiation. Stock certificates inherited this materiality: a piece
  of paper that is not merely a record of ownership but the legal
  instrument of ownership itself. The physicality of the tree trunk
  persists in the insistence that financial instruments be tangible,
  holdable, real.
- **Splitting creates accountability** -- the genius of the tally stick
  was that splitting it created two unique halves that could only match
  each other. This maps onto the fundamental structure of double-entry
  bookkeeping and the bilateral nature of financial contracts. Every
  stock transaction has two sides: buyer and seller, each holding their
  half of the split.
- **Solidity and permanence** -- a tree trunk is stable, rooted, enduring.
  "Stock" carries this connotation into finance: stock is the permanent
  capital of a company, as opposed to flow (revenue, expenses). The
  distinction between stocks and flows in economics preserves the
  original contrast between the solid trunk and the moving sap.
- **Rootedness as origin** -- "stock" also means lineage (as in
  "laughingstock," "from good stock"). The tree trunk is what everything
  else grows from. A company's stock is its root capital, the trunk
  from which all branches of activity extend.

## Where It Breaks

- **Wood is tangible; equity is abstract** -- a modern share of stock
  exists as a database entry at a central depository. There is no
  physical object, no piece of wood, no certificate in most cases. The
  materiality that gave the metaphor its force has been entirely
  dematerialized. When stock is traded in microseconds by algorithms,
  the tree trunk is unimaginably distant.
- **Tally sticks recorded debt; stock represents ownership** -- the
  original tally stick was a debt instrument: someone owed something.
  Modern stock represents equity ownership, not debt. The metaphor
  shifted from "what you are owed" to "what you own" -- a fundamental
  inversion that the word silently absorbed.
- **The split no longer requires matching** -- tally sticks worked because
  the two halves had to be physically reunited. Modern stock trades
  require no such physical reconciliation. The clearing system is
  abstract, electronic, and operates on trust in institutions rather
  than trust in wood grain. The metaphor's original mechanism of
  verification has no analogue.
- **"Stock" now means too many things** -- livestock, inventory,
  laughingstock, rolling stock, stock photography. The tree-trunk origin
  branched so promiscuously that the word has lost all metaphorical
  coherence. Each usage preserves a different aspect of the original
  (solidity, supply, rootedness, material), but no single user holds
  all these meanings simultaneously.

## Expressions

- "Stock market" -- a market for pieces of split wood, now the central
  institution of global capitalism
- "Stockholder" -- one who holds the larger half of the tally, now one
  who owns equity in a corporation
- "Stock exchange" -- where tally sticks were matched and debts settled,
  now where ownership is transferred electronically
- "Take stock" -- to count one's inventory (from the trunk-as-supply
  meaning), metaphorically extended to mean any assessment of one's
  situation
- "Lock, stock, and barrel" -- the three components of a musket,
  where "stock" is the wooden body -- the tree trunk in its most
  literal martial form
- "Stub" -- the debtor's smaller half of the tally, surviving in
  ticket stubs, checkbook stubs, and pay stubs -- all remnants of a
  split record

## Origin Story

The Exchequer of medieval England ran on tally sticks. From the 12th
century until 1826, the English government recorded tax payments by
notching a piece of hazelwood and splitting it. The stock (from *stocc*,
tree trunk) was the larger half, kept by the creditor -- originally the
Crown. The foil (or stub) went to the taxpayer. When Parliament finally
abolished the system in 1826, the accumulated wooden tallies were stored
in the Houses of Parliament. In 1834, someone decided to burn the
obsolete tallies in the furnaces beneath the House of Lords. The fire
got out of control and burned both Houses of Parliament to the ground.

The London Stock Exchange literally began with these wooden instruments.
"Stocks" were government debt recorded on tally sticks. When transferable
shares in joint-stock companies emerged in the 17th century, the word
"stock" transferred with them, carrying the materiality of wood into the
abstraction of equity. By the time the New York Stock Exchange was
founded in 1792, nobody was splitting sticks anymore, but the word
persisted, a linguistic fossil of medieval accounting technology.
