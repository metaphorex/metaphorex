## What It Brings

A problem holds its solution inside it, locked away. To solve the problem
is to find the key, open the container, and extract what is within. This
metaphor maps the container image schema onto the relationship between a
problem and its resolution, producing a specific cognitive structure: the
solution is not elsewhere, not invented, not assembled from parts -- it is
already present, sealed inside the problem itself, waiting to be released.

Key structural parallels:

- **The problem as enclosure** -- "The answer is in there somewhere." "I
  can't crack this problem." "There must be a way into this." The problem
  is conceptualized as a bounded, closed space. It has an inside (where
  the solution resides) and an outside (where the solver stands). This
  framing gives problems a spatial structure: they have interiors that
  can be explored, boundaries that resist entry, and openings that can
  be discovered or forced.

- **The solution as contents** -- "I need to extract the answer." "The
  solution was buried in the data." "Once you open it up, the answer is
  obvious." The solution preexists as a thing inside the container. It
  is not created by the solver but found, uncovered, or liberated. This
  maps the container's contents onto the resolution of the problem.

- **Solving as unlocking or opening** -- "She cracked the code." "He
  found the key to the puzzle." "We finally broke it open." The act of
  solving requires overcoming the container's resistance -- its lock, its
  seal, its structural integrity. This gives problem-solving a specific
  dramatic shape: sustained effort against a barrier followed by sudden
  access.

- **Difficulty as impermeability** -- "It's impenetrable." "I can't get
  inside this problem." "The answer is locked away." The harder the
  problem, the stronger the container. Difficulty is mapped onto the
  container's resistance to being opened, which makes problem difficulty
  feel like a material property rather than a cognitive one.

## Where It Breaks

- **Not all solutions preexist within problems** -- the metaphor assumes
  the answer is already there, sealed inside, needing only to be found.
  But many problems require the solution to be invented, negotiated, or
  constructed from external resources. Design problems, ethical dilemmas,
  and political disputes rarely have solutions waiting inside them. The
  metaphor biases toward discovery over creation, which can make people
  feel they are "missing something" when the real task is to build
  something new.

- **The metaphor hides partial solutions** -- a container is either open
  or closed; the contents are either accessible or not. This binary
  structure obscures the reality of partial progress, approximate
  answers, and good-enough resolutions. In practice, most problem-solving
  involves incremental understanding, not a single moment of unlocking.

- **It privileges individual insight** -- the locked-container model
  favors the lone solver who finds the key. Collaborative problem-solving,
  where multiple people contribute partial understanding that collectively
  opens the problem, does not map cleanly. The metaphor has one lock and
  one key, not a combination requiring multiple hands.

- **The metaphor implies problems are self-contained** -- if the solution
  is inside the problem, then everything you need is there. But most
  real-world problems are entangled with other problems, and solving one
  often requires information, resources, or changes that originate
  entirely outside the problem's boundaries.

- **It makes unsolvable problems feel like failures of effort** -- if the
  solution is in there, the only reason you have not found it is that you
  have not tried hard enough or found the right key. Some problems are
  genuinely unsolvable, ill-defined, or dissolved rather than solved.
  The container metaphor has no vocabulary for this.

## Expressions

- "Crack the problem" -- forcing open a sealed container
- "Unlock the mystery" -- finding the key to a locked enclosure
- "The key to the problem" -- the specific means of opening the container
- "Break it open" -- overcoming the container's resistance by force
- "Get inside the problem" -- entering the container to access its
  contents
- "The answer is in there somewhere" -- the solution as hidden contents
- "It's impenetrable" -- the container resists all attempts at opening
- "Unpack the issue" -- opening the container and examining its contents
- "Pry it open" -- effortful opening of a resistant enclosure
- "The solution was buried in the details" -- contents concealed within
  a deep container

## Origin Story

This metaphor appears in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson &
Schwartz, 1991) as a specific elaboration of the general CONTAINER image
schema applied to problems and their resolutions. It builds on Lakoff
and Johnson's foundational claim in *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) that
containment is one of the most basic structuring schemas in human
cognition: we understand categories, states, and situations as containers
with insides and outsides. Applying this schema to problems produces a
distinctive model of problem-solving as penetration and extraction --
a model deeply embedded in Western intellectual tradition, from puzzle
boxes to cryptography to the "eureka" moment of scientific discovery.
