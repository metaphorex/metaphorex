## What It Brings

Some beliefs are "in" and others are "out." Intellectual positions come
into style, enjoy a period of dominance, and then fall out of fashion --
not because they were refuted but because the culture moved on. BELIEFS
ARE FASHIONS maps the cyclical, social, and fundamentally non-rational
dynamics of clothing trends onto the adoption and abandonment of ideas.

Key structural parallels:

- **Beliefs as garments** -- you "wear" your beliefs, and they are visible
  to others. Adopting a belief is like putting on an outfit: it signals
  group membership and personal identity. "She wears her feminism on her
  sleeve" fuses belief-display with clothing-display.
- **Trendiness as intellectual currency** -- beliefs go "in vogue" and
  "out of fashion." This captures something that pure epistemology
  cannot: the social dynamics of belief adoption. People hold certain
  views partly because those views are currently popular in their
  reference group, not because they have independently evaluated the
  evidence.
- **Fashion cycles as intellectual cycles** -- ideas that were once
  dismissed come back around. Keynesian economics, vinyl records,
  behaviorism in psychology -- the metaphor recognizes that intellectual
  history is not a linear march of progress but a cycle of trends.
  What's "retro" in clothing has an analogue in "rediscovering" old
  theoretical frameworks.
- **Designers as thought leaders** -- just as fashion has trendsetters
  and followers, intellectual life has people who originate ideas and
  people who adopt them. The metaphor highlights the social hierarchy
  of belief: some people set the agenda and others follow, regardless
  of the epistemic merits.

## Where It Breaks

- **Fashion is explicitly non-rational; beliefs claim rationality** --
  nobody pretends that hemline length is determined by evidence. But
  beliefs are supposed to be held for reasons, and the fashion metaphor
  implicitly accuses believers of unreflective trend-following. Using
  the metaphor is inherently dismissive: to call a belief "fashionable"
  is to question its rational basis. This makes the metaphor a weapon
  more than a description.
- **Fashion is temporary by design; some beliefs endure** -- the fashion
  frame predicts that every trend will pass. But some beliefs (the Earth
  orbits the Sun, slavery is wrong) have become permanent features of
  the intellectual landscape. The metaphor cannot distinguish a fad from
  a discovery and implicitly treats all beliefs as equally transient.
- **The metaphor obscures evidence** -- if beliefs are fashions, their
  truth value is irrelevant. This is useful for describing the sociology
  of belief, but dangerous as an epistemology. Climate science is not
  "in fashion" -- it is supported by evidence. The fashion metaphor
  flattens the difference between well-supported and merely popular.
- **Individual conviction vanishes** -- in the fashion frame, nobody
  holds a belief because they genuinely think it is true; they hold it
  because it is "in." The metaphor erases the possibility of principled,
  evidence-based commitment that happens to align with a trend. Being
  right and being fashionable become indistinguishable.

## Expressions

- "That theory is very fashionable right now" -- a belief as a
  currently popular style
- "Marxism went out of fashion in the 1990s" -- a belief system as
  last season's collection
- "She's a trendsetter in the field" -- an intellectual leader as a
  fashion designer
- "That idea is so last century" -- an outdated belief as outdated
  clothing
- "He follows whatever intellectual fad comes along" -- uncritical
  belief adoption as trend-chasing
- "Behaviorism is making a comeback" -- a revived theory as a retro
  fashion trend
- "Old-fashioned values" -- traditional beliefs as vintage clothing
- "Fashionable nonsense" (Sokal & Bricmont) -- the explicit weaponization
  of the metaphor against postmodern theory
- "The marketplace of ideas" -- partially overlapping metaphor where
  beliefs compete like products for consumer attention

## Origin Story

BELIEFS ARE FASHIONS appears in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson
& Schwartz, 1991) as one of several related metaphors for belief (alongside
BELIEFS ARE POSSESSIONS, BELIEFS ARE LOCATIONS, and BELIEFS ARE GUIDES).
The fashion variant specifically targets the social and cyclical nature of
belief adoption, distinguishing it from the other belief metaphors, which
emphasize ownership (possessions), commitment (locations), or authority
(guides). The metaphor has been productively used in the sociology of
knowledge, particularly by critics who want to highlight the non-rational
forces shaping intellectual trends. Sokal and Bricmont's *Fashionable
Nonsense* (1998) is a prominent example of the metaphor deployed
polemically.
