## What It Brings

After ten years of failed siege, the Greeks built a large wooden horse,
hid soldiers inside it, and left it at the gates of Troy as an apparent
offering. The Trojans, believing the Greeks had departed, brought the
horse inside the city walls. That night the hidden soldiers emerged,
opened the gates, and Troy fell. The metaphor maps this structure --
something that appears benign or desirable but conceals a hidden threat
-- onto any situation where a trusted exterior disguises hostile intent.

- **Trust is the attack vector** -- the horse succeeded because the
  Trojans trusted the gift. They had to actively choose to bring it
  inside the walls. The metaphor imports the structure of an attack
  that exploits trust rather than force: the threat is not breaking
  through defenses but persuading the defender to voluntarily lower
  them. This maps directly onto social engineering in cybersecurity,
  phishing emails disguised as legitimate correspondence, malware
  bundled with legitimate software, and any vector where the target's
  own decision-making is the vulnerability.
- **The exterior is desirable** -- the horse was not merely harmless-
  looking; it was attractive. It appeared to be a sacred offering to
  Athena. The Trojans did not reluctantly accept it; they celebrated
  it. The metaphor imports the structure of an attack that is actively
  welcomed by its target. This maps onto free software that bundles
  spyware, corporate acquisitions that conceal hostile takeover
  intentions, political legislation that embeds harmful provisions
  in popular bills, and any context where the dangerous payload is
  wrapped in something the recipient genuinely wants.
- **The defenses are intact but irrelevant** -- Troy's walls were
  never breached. The greatest fortification in the ancient world
  was rendered meaningless because the threat came from inside. The
  metaphor imports the structure of security bypass: not defeating
  the defenses but making them irrelevant by getting the payload
  past them voluntarily. This maps onto firewalls that cannot stop
  threats that arrive as legitimate traffic, immune systems that
  cannot fight pathogens disguised as the body's own cells, and
  any defense system that is designed for external threats but
  vulnerable to internal ones.
- **The attack is deferred** -- the soldiers waited inside the horse
  until nightfall. The threat did not activate immediately upon entry;
  it was dormant during the period when it might have been detected.
  This maps onto time-delayed malware, sleeper agents, contractual
  clauses that activate under specific conditions, and any payload
  that separates the moment of infiltration from the moment of action.

## Where It Breaks

- **The Trojans were warned** -- Laocoon said "I fear Greeks bearing
  gifts" and hurled a spear at the horse. Cassandra prophesied
  destruction. The myth includes explicit, ignored warnings. But the
  metaphor, as commonly used, implies that the deception was
  undetectable. Real Trojan horse attacks are often discussed as if
  they were unforeseeable, when in fact -- like the original -- there
  were usually people who raised alarms that were ignored. The
  metaphor can obscure the role of institutional failure to heed
  warnings by framing the attack as inherently undetectable.
- **In cybersecurity, "Trojan" has detached entirely from the myth**
  -- a "Trojan horse" in computing is simply malware that disguises
  itself as legitimate software. The term is so thoroughly dead as a
  metaphor that security professionals use "Trojan" as a technical
  category, not a literary allusion. Many practitioners who discuss
  Trojans daily could not recount the myth of the wooden horse at
  Troy. The metaphor has completed the dead-metaphor lifecycle: it
  has become a literal term in its target domain.
- **The metaphor assumes a single, intentional attacker** -- Odysseus
  designed the horse with a specific plan and a specific target. But
  many real-world Trojan structures are not deliberately designed by
  a single adversary. A software dependency that introduces
  vulnerabilities is often not maliciously crafted; it is poorly
  maintained, inadequately reviewed, or compromised after the fact
  by a third party. The metaphor's narrative of deliberate cunning
  maps poorly onto supply chain vulnerabilities that emerge from
  systemic neglect rather than intentional deception.
- **The horse was a one-time trick** -- it worked because it had
  never been tried before. Troy had no precedent for this kind of
  attack. But real Trojan strategies are used repeatedly, and
  defenders learn to recognize them. Phishing awareness training,
  malware scanning, code review, and supply chain auditing are all
  responses to the repeated use of the Trojan structure. The myth
  offers no model for the iterative arms race between Trojan
  attacks and Trojan defenses because the original was a singular,
  unrepeatable event.
- **The metaphor frames the victim as foolish** -- "don't be like
  the Trojans" is the implicit moral. But the Trojans' decision
  was not irrational given their information and beliefs. They had
  religious reasons to accept a divine offering; refusing it might
  anger the gods. The metaphor, in its modern use, strips away the
  cultural and epistemic context that made the Trojans' decision
  reasonable and replaces it with hindsight-biased contempt for
  the victim. This maps onto the cybersecurity tendency to blame
  users who click phishing links, ignoring the design failures
  that made the phish convincing.

## Expressions

- "Trojan horse" -- the standard idiom for anything that appears
  beneficial but conceals a hidden threat, used across technology,
  politics, business, and everyday speech
- "Trojan" -- in computing, the shortened form that functions as a
  technical category name for malware that disguises itself as
  legitimate software, fully detached from its mythological origin
- "Beware of Greeks bearing gifts" -- from Virgil's *Aeneid*
  (Laocoon's warning "timeo Danaos et dona ferentes"), the
  proverbial form advising suspicion of unexpectedly generous offers
- "Trojan horse legislation" -- in politics, a bill that contains
  hidden provisions that serve a different purpose than the bill's
  stated intent
- "The Greeks are inside the walls" -- describing the moment when
  a concealed threat has already penetrated defenses and it is too
  late for perimeter security, used in incident response contexts

## Origin Story

The Trojan Horse appears in Homer's *Odyssey* (c. 8th century BCE)
by reference -- Odysseus weeps when the bard Demodocus sings of it
-- and is described more fully in Virgil's *Aeneid* (19 BCE), Book
II, where Aeneas recounts the fall of Troy. The fullest ancient
account is in Quintus Smyrnaeus's *Posthomerica* (c. 4th century CE).
The stratagem is attributed to Odysseus, though some sources credit
Athena with the idea.

The phrase "Trojan horse" entered English as a general metaphor for
concealed threats by the 16th century. Its application to computing
began in 1974, when Daniel Edwards used the term in a US Air Force
report on computer security to describe a program that performs a
desired function but also contains hidden malicious code. By the
1980s, "Trojan" was an established category in computer security
taxonomy, and by the 2000s it had become one of the most commonly
used terms in cybersecurity -- fully lexicalized and no longer
experienced as a metaphor by most of its users.

## References

- Homer. *Odyssey* (c. 8th century BCE) -- the earliest surviving
  reference to the wooden horse, mentioned in Books 4, 8, and 11
- Virgil. *Aeneid*, Book II (19 BCE) -- the most influential
  narrative of the horse and the fall of Troy, source of "timeo
  Danaos et dona ferentes"
- Quintus Smyrnaeus. *Posthomerica* (c. 4th century CE) -- the
  most detailed ancient account of the horse's construction and
  the soldiers hidden inside
- Edwards, Daniel. "Computer Security Technology Planning Study"
  (1974) -- the first documented use of "Trojan horse" as a
  computing term, in a US Air Force report
- Thompson, Ken. "Reflections on Trusting Trust" (1984) -- the
  classic paper on compiler-level Trojan attacks, demonstrating
  how deeply the Trojan structure can be embedded in software systems
