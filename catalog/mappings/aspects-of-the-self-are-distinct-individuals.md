## What It Brings

We talk about ourselves as if we are committees. "Part of me wants to go,
but another part of me knows better." "I had to fight myself to get out
of bed." "My better angels prevailed." The single self is conceptualized
as a collection of distinct agents -- sub-selves with their own desires,
voices, and agendas -- who argue, cooperate, and struggle for control.

Key structural parallels:

- **Internal conflict as interpersonal conflict** -- when desires clash,
  we experience it as two people disagreeing. "I'm at war with myself"
  maps an internal psychological state onto an external social scene.
  The metaphor gives internal conflict a dramatic structure: protagonist,
  antagonist, and resolution.
- **Distinct roles for distinct functions** -- the "inner child," the
  "inner critic," the "voice of reason," the "devil on your shoulder."
  Each psychological function gets personified as a separate individual
  with a recognizable social role. The metaphor imports the structure of
  social relationships (authority, rebellion, nurturing, criticism) into
  the psyche.
- **Dialogue as deliberation** -- thinking things through becomes a
  conversation between selves. "I told myself to calm down." "Something
  inside me said no." Internal deliberation is externalized as speech
  acts between distinct speakers. This makes private cognition amenable
  to narrative description.
- **Dominance as self-control** -- when one sub-self prevails, that is
  "being in control." When the wrong sub-self wins, that is "losing
  control" or "giving in to temptation." The metaphor maps the social
  hierarchy of persons onto the internal hierarchy of motivations:
  reason should govern desire, the adult self should overrule the
  childish self.

## Where It Breaks

- **The self is not actually plural** -- the metaphor reifies internal
  states as agents, but there is no evidence for discrete sub-personalities
  in typical cognition. The "inner child" is not a child; the "inner
  critic" is not a person. By treating aspects of experience as separate
  beings, the metaphor can encourage disowning parts of oneself: "That
  wasn't really me" becomes a way of avoiding responsibility for actions
  that emerged from one's own desires.
- **The metaphor obscures integration** -- healthy psychological
  functioning may involve integration of competing impulses, not the
  victory of one sub-self over another. But the social-roles frame
  imports a win-lose structure: someone has to prevail. The metaphor
  makes it hard to describe a state where conflicting desires are held
  simultaneously without resolution.
- **It pathologizes normal ambivalence** -- if the self is a collection
  of individuals, then internal disagreement looks like dysfunction.
  "I can't make up my mind" becomes a failure of governance rather than
  a natural response to genuine uncertainty. The metaphor implies that a
  well-ordered self, like a well-ordered society, should speak with one
  voice.
- **Cultural load varies** -- the Western tradition of reason-versus-
  passion (Plato's charioteer, Freud's id/ego/superego) reinforces this
  metaphor heavily, but other cultural frameworks understand the self
  differently. Buddhist psychology, for instance, treats the unified self
  as the illusion and moment-to-moment experience as primary, inverting
  the metaphor's assumptions.

## Expressions

- "Part of me wants to stay" -- one sub-self has a distinct desire
- "I had to force myself" -- one self coercing another into action
- "My heart says yes but my head says no" -- bodily organs as distinct
  advisors
- "I'm at war with myself" -- internal conflict as armed combat between
  selves
- "The devil on my shoulder" -- temptation as a separate person offering
  counsel
- "My better angels" -- Lincoln's phrase, moral impulses as distinct
  beings
- "I couldn't help myself" -- one self unable to restrain another
- "Something inside me just snapped" -- a sub-self breaking free of
  control
- "Listen to your inner voice" -- a sub-self as a speaker with wisdom
- "The inner child" -- developmental psychology's personification of
  early-life emotional patterns

## Origin Story

This metaphor is documented in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson
& Schwartz, 1991) as part of the SELF system of metaphors. Lakoff and
Johnson develop it in *Philosophy in the Flesh* (1999) as one of several
related metaphors for the self, alongside THE SELF IS A CONTAINER and THE
SELF IS A PHYSICAL OBJECT. The intellectual roots are far older: Plato's
tripartite soul (reason, spirit, appetite as three distinct agents in the
*Republic*), Freud's structural model (id, ego, superego), and the
Christian tradition of the soul struggling against the flesh all
instantiate versions of this mapping. What Lakoff and Johnson added was
the recognition that this is not just a philosophical theory but a
pervasive feature of everyday language -- ordinary speakers who have never
read Plato routinely talk about themselves as multiple agents.
