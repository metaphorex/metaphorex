## What It Brings

Anger raises body temperature. The face flushes, the blood runs hot, the
skin burns. This physiological correlation is the grounding for one of
the most systematic emotion metaphors in English (and across many other
languages). ANGER IS HEAT maps the thermal domain onto the experience of
anger, producing an elaborate and coherent system of expressions.

Key structural parallels:

- **Rising temperature as rising anger** -- anger "builds," "mounts,"
  and "heats up." The metaphor captures the temporal profile of anger:
  it is not instantaneous but escalates, and the thermal scale provides
  a natural measure of intensity. You can be "a little hot under the
  collar" or "boiling with rage."
- **Boiling point as loss of control** -- the most important mapping.
  There is a critical threshold where heat produces a phase change
  (liquid to gas, or in the extended metaphor, calm to explosion).
  "Blowing your top," "boiling over," and "letting off steam" all
  reference this threshold. The metaphor implies that anger has a
  container, and past a certain point, pressure exceeds containment.
- **Heat source as cause of anger** -- whatever provokes anger is
  what "gets you heated." The cause is a flame, a spark, or a source
  of friction. "That really burns me up" locates the cause as a heat
  source acting on the angry person.
- **Cooling as calming** -- "cool down," "let it simmer," "take a cold
  shower." The antidote to anger is the removal of heat. Cooling is
  not just metaphorical; people genuinely seek cold water, fresh air,
  and spatial distance when angry, reinforcing the embodied grounding.

This metaphor composes with ANGER IS A HEATED FLUID IN A CONTAINER
(a more specific mapping) where the body is the container, anger is the
fluid, and the escalation follows the physics of pressurized liquids.

## Where It Breaks

- **Heat is physical; anger is evaluative** -- the metaphor captures the
  phenomenology of anger (it feels hot) but obscures its cognitive
  content. Anger is always anger *about* something -- a perceived
  injustice, a violated expectation, a blocked goal. The heat metaphor
  has no way to represent what the anger is about, only how intense it
  is. You can be equally "hot" about a parking ticket and a betrayal.
- **The boiling-point model implies inevitability** -- the physics of
  heat suggests that if enough provocation accumulates, an explosion is
  unavoidable. This naturalizes loss of control: "I just snapped" treats
  the angry person as a thermodynamic system rather than a moral agent.
  The metaphor provides a ready-made excuse for rage.
- **Cooling implies suppression, not resolution** -- "cool down" means
  reduce the temperature, not address the cause. The metaphor's remedy
  (remove heat) maps onto anger management strategies that prioritize
  calm over justice. You can cool someone down without addressing what
  made them angry.
- **The metaphor is less apt for slow-burn resentment** -- ANGER IS HEAT
  works best for acute, escalating anger. Long-term resentment, which
  may be cold and calculating ("a dish best served cold" -- that's
  revenge, a different mapping), does not fit the thermal model.
  Sustained low-grade anger is thermally incoherent.

## Expressions

- "She was boiling with rage" -- extreme anger as liquid at boiling point
- "He blew his top" -- loss of control as pressure exceeding containment
- "Don't get heated" -- warning against emotional escalation as thermal
  escalation
- "That really burns me up" -- provocation as heat applied to the body
- "Let off some steam" -- controlled release of anger as venting pressure
- "He's a hothead" -- person prone to anger as one who runs hot
- "Cool down" -- calming as temperature reduction
- "Hot under the collar" -- moderate anger as localized heat
- "A heated argument" -- angry exchange as thermally elevated interaction
- "Simmer down" -- transition from boiling to calmer state
- "She was fuming" -- anger producing visible heat (smoke, fumes)

## Origin Story

ANGER IS HEAT appears in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson &
Schwartz, 1991) and is analyzed in detail by Lakoff (1987) and Kovecses
(1986, 2000). Lakoff's analysis in *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things*
demonstrates that the metaphor is not a loose collection of expressions
but a coherent system with internal logic: anger is a hot fluid, the body
is a container, intensity is temperature, and loss of control is the
fluid exceeding the container's capacity. Kovecses extended the analysis
cross-linguistically, finding ANGER IS HEAT in Chinese, Japanese,
Hungarian, and Zulu, though the specific elaborations differ (in Chinese,
anger rises as *qi* rather than as steam). The cross-linguistic prevalence
supports the claim that the metaphor is grounded in universal physiology
-- the autonomic nervous system response that raises skin temperature
during anger.
