## What It Brings

To cause something is to control where it goes. This metaphor structures
causation as the exercise of authority over an entity's position in a
spatial field: the causer is a controller who moves, places, keeps, or
removes an entity relative to a location, and the effect is the entity's
resulting position. It is part of the Event Structure metaphor system's
"location case," where states are locations, changes of state are
movements, and causes are the agents who make those movements happen.

Key structural parallels:

- **Causing a state as putting something in a place** -- "She put him
  in a difficult position." "The policy placed the economy in jeopardy."
  "He got himself into trouble." To cause someone to be in a state is to
  move them into the location that represents that state. The causer has
  the power to pick up the entity and set it down somewhere new.
- **Preventing change as keeping something in place** -- "She held the
  team together." "He kept the project on track." "The regulations held
  prices in check." To prevent a change of state is to prevent an
  entity from leaving its current location. The causer's power is
  exercised as restraint: holding, pinning, anchoring.
- **Enabling as releasing** -- "She let him go." "The reform freed up
  capital." "He unleashed a chain of events." To enable a change is to
  release an entity from the location where it has been held. The
  causer's power is exercised by removing a constraint rather than
  applying a force.
- **Causing change as moving something between locations** -- "The
  treatment brought him from despair to hope." "The crisis pushed the
  country from stability into chaos." "She moved the conversation from
  small talk to substance." State change is transit between locations,
  and the causer is the agent who orchestrates the transit.
- **Degree of control as spatial precision** -- "He put it exactly
  where he wanted it." "She couldn't quite get the tone right." "They
  placed the blame squarely on management." Fine-grained control over
  effects is mapped onto precise spatial placement; clumsy causation
  is imprecise positioning.

## Where It Breaks

- **Causation is not always agentive** -- the metaphor requires a
  controller, an agent who exercises authority over the entity's
  location. But many causes are not agents: gravity causes objects
  to fall, inflation causes prices to rise, aging causes decline.
  The metaphor imports intentionality and authority into causal
  relations that may be entirely impersonal, which can lead to
  spurious attribution of blame or credit.
- **States are not actually locations** -- this metaphor inherits the
  limitations of STATES ARE LOCATIONS. If the target state is not
  well-modeled as a bounded region (and many states are not -- think
  of gradual transitions, mixed states, or states with no clear
  boundaries), then the control-over-location model of causation
  loses its spatial logic.
- **The metaphor suggests discrete placement** -- "putting" something
  in a state implies a moment of placement, a discrete transition.
  But many causal processes are gradual, continuous, and distributed
  over time. A person does not get "put into" depression the way a
  book gets put on a shelf; the causation is diffuse and incremental.
- **Control implies reversibility** -- if you can put something in a
  location, you can presumably take it out again. But many causal
  effects are irreversible. The metaphor suggests a degree of
  controllability that the actual causal situation may not support,
  which can generate false optimism about the ability to undo effects
  simply by exercising the same authority in reverse.
- **It obscures structural and systemic causes** -- the
  controller-and-entity model focuses attention on a single agent
  causing a change in a single entity. This makes it hard to represent
  systemic causation, where no individual agent has control and the
  effect emerges from the interaction of many factors. "Who put the
  economy in recession?" presupposes a controller who may not exist.

## Expressions

- "She put him in a difficult position" -- causing a state as placing
  in a location
- "He got himself into trouble" -- self-causation as self-placement
- "The policy held prices in check" -- prevention as spatial restraint
- "She kept the team together" -- maintaining a state as keeping
  entities in place
- "The crisis pushed them into poverty" -- causation as forced
  movement between locations
- "He was placed under suspicion" -- causing a cognitive state as
  placing in a region
- "She freed him from his obligations" -- enabling as releasing from
  a location
- "The treatment brought him back from the brink" -- reversing a
  state change as returning to a prior location
- "They moved the goalposts" -- changing the conditions for success
  as relocating the destination

## Origin Story

This metaphor is documented in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff,
Espenson & Schwartz, 1991) as part of the Event Structure metaphor
system, specifically the "location case." The Event Structure system
was Lakoff's attempt to show that our entire understanding of events,
causes, states, and changes is structured by a small set of interlocking
spatial metaphors. CAUSATION IS CONTROL OVER AN ENTITY RELATIVE TO A
LOCATION is the causation component of the location case: it explains
how we talk about making things happen by mapping the causer onto a
controller who moves entities between the spatial locations that represent
states.

The metaphor works in tandem with STATES ARE LOCATIONS and CHANGE IS
MOTION to form a coherent system: states are places, changes are
movements between places, and causes are the agents who control those
movements. Lakoff developed this analysis most fully in *Philosophy in
the Flesh* (1999), where the Event Structure system is presented as one
of the most fundamental metaphor systems in human cognition.
