## What It Brings

One of the most deeply grounded metaphors in all of cognitive linguistics.
Physical warmth maps onto interpersonal affection with a directness that
most speakers never notice, because the correlation between the two is
established before language: infants experience warmth simultaneously with
being held by a caregiver. The co-occurrence of bodily heat and parental
love creates a primary scene (in Grady's sense) that persists into adult
cognition.

Key structural parallels:

- **Temperature as emotional register** -- warm people are friendly, cold
  people are distant. The thermal scale maps directly onto a scale of
  interpersonal closeness, with "warm" at the positive end and "cold" at
  the negative end. There is no neutral temperature; lukewarm implies
  indifference, which itself registers as a mild negative.
- **Heat sources as affection sources** -- a "warm home" is one filled with
  love, not just one with good insulation. A "warm welcome" is emotionally
  generous. The source of warmth and the source of affection are fused.
- **Coldness as emotional withdrawal** -- giving someone "the cold shoulder,"
  an "icy stare," a "frosty reception." The absence of warmth does not
  merely indicate neutral affect; it implies active rejection. Cold is not
  just the absence of warmth but the presence of hostility.
- **Shared warmth as intimacy** -- "warming up to someone" describes the
  gradual development of trust and familiarity. The metaphor captures the
  temporal dimension of relationship-building: people do not go from cold
  to hot instantaneously, and the slow increase in temperature maps onto
  the slow increase in closeness.

## Where It Breaks

- **Warmth is symmetrical; affection often is not** -- two bodies near each
  other share heat equally, but affection is frequently unreciprocated. The
  metaphor has no good way to represent one-sided warmth. "She was warm to
  him, but he was cold" works, but it treats the two parties as independent
  heat sources rather than capturing the relational asymmetry.
- **The metaphor flattens the variety of affection** -- warmth is a single
  scalar dimension (more or less hot), but affection comes in many forms:
  parental love, romantic desire, friendship, professional regard, religious
  devotion. "Warm" covers all of them, which is economical but imprecise.
  A warm handshake and a warm embrace are not the same kind of affection.
- **Excessive heat is not excessive affection** -- the metaphor works at
  moderate temperatures, but breaks at extremes. "Burning love" borrows
  from a different metaphor (DESIRE IS FIRE / LOVE IS FIRE) with different
  entailments -- danger, consumption, destruction. The warmth metaphor
  cannot scale to intensity without importing a different source domain.
- **Cultural variation in temperature norms** -- what counts as "warm"
  behavior varies across cultures. The metaphor is grounded in universal
  embodied experience (infants everywhere are held), but its linguistic
  expressions differ. In some cultures, emotional restraint is the norm,
  and what English speakers call "cold" might register as appropriately
  respectful.

## Expressions

- "She's a warm person" -- affectionate temperament as high temperature
- "He gave her the cold shoulder" -- emotional rejection as withdrawal of
  heat
- "A warm welcome" -- hospitality as thermal comfort
- "Icy stare" -- hostility as extreme cold
- "Warming up to someone" -- gradual trust as gradual temperature increase
- "She left me cold" -- failure to create emotional connection as failure
  to transmit heat
- "A frosty reception" -- social hostility as sub-zero temperature
- "They have a warm relationship" -- ongoing affection as sustained heat
- "Cold comfort" -- consolation that fails to provide emotional warmth

## Origin Story

Lakoff and Johnson discuss the correlation of warmth and affection in
*Metaphors We Live By* (1980) as an example of metaphor grounded in
physical experience. Grady (1997) later formalized it as a primary metaphor
-- one arising from a "primary scene" in which subjective experience
(affection) regularly co-occurs with sensorimotor experience (warmth)
during early childhood. The metaphor appears in *Philosophy in the Flesh*
(1999) in Lakoff and Johnson's catalog of primary metaphors. Williams and
Bargh (2008) provided experimental evidence: subjects holding a warm cup
of coffee rated a stranger as having a "warmer" personality than subjects
holding an iced coffee, suggesting the mapping operates below conscious
awareness.
