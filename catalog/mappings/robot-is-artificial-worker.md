## What It Brings

The word "robot" entered the world through Karel Capek's 1920 play *R.U.R.
(Rossum's Universal Robots)*, where it was coined from the Czech "robota,"
meaning forced labor or serfdom. Capek's robots were not mechanical; they
were synthetic biological beings manufactured to perform the work that
humans found degrading. The word has since become the default term for any
autonomous mechanical or software agent -- so thoroughly naturalized that
its labor-and-servitude origin has vanished from common awareness.

Key structural parallels:

- **Creation for servitude** -- the foundational import of the metaphor is
  that artificial agents exist to serve. A robot is not a peer, a partner,
  or a citizen; it is a worker, built to labor at its creator's command.
  This structures how societies think about automation: machines are
  instruments of production, and the question is always how much human
  labor they can replace.
- **The replaceability of the worker** -- "robota" carried connotations of
  expendable, interchangeable labor. The metaphor imports this onto
  machines: robots are mass-produced, individually worthless, and designed
  to be discarded when worn out. This framing shapes industrial robot
  design (standardized, modular, replaceable) and software agent design
  (stateless, ephemeral, fungible).
- **The rebellion of the created** -- Capek's play ends with the robots
  revolting against their human creators. This narrative import has proven
  as durable as the word itself: from Frankenstein to Terminator to
  contemporary AI safety discourse, the expectation that created beings
  will turn on their creators is built into the metaphor. Every
  conversation about AI alignment carries an echo of Capek's factory floor.
- **The erasure of the worker's interiority** -- a robot, by etymological
  definition, is pure function. It works. The metaphor suppresses questions
  about the machine's experience, preferences, or rights. When AI systems
  become sophisticated enough to simulate preferences, the robot framing
  provides no vocabulary for engaging with that simulation -- it can only
  see a worker, not a subject.

## Where It Breaks

- **Modern AI systems do not labor** -- a language model generating text is
  not performing forced work in any meaningful sense. It has no preferences
  to be overridden, no fatigue to accumulate, no freedom to be denied. The
  labor metaphor imports a moral framework (exploitation, rights, dignity)
  that requires a suffering subject. Applying it to software systems either
  trivializes actual labor exploitation or attributes experience to machines
  that almost certainly lack it.
- **The servitude frame obscures collaboration** -- framing AI as a worker
  makes human-AI interaction a command relationship: the human directs, the
  machine obeys. This misses the actual dynamics of working with modern AI
  systems, where the interaction is more like a conversation than an order.
  The robot metaphor cannot see pair programming, co-creation, or
  human-in-the-loop workflows.
- **Capek's robots were biological, not mechanical** -- the original
  "robots" were made of synthetic flesh, closer to clones than to machines.
  The word's migration from biological to mechanical to software has
  stretched the metaphor across three entirely different substrates, each
  time losing more of the original meaning. A software bot called "robot"
  shares nothing with Capek's creation except the name.
- **The rebellion narrative distorts risk assessment** -- the expectation
  that AI will "rebel" imports a narrative of intentional opposition that
  misframes actual AI risks. Real AI dangers involve misalignment (the
  system optimizing for the wrong objective), misuse (humans deploying AI
  harmfully), and brittleness (the system failing in unexpected ways) --
  none of which resemble a slave uprising. The robot metaphor makes AI
  risk dramatic and cinematic when it is actually mundane and systemic.

## Expressions

- "Robot" -- any autonomous mechanical or software agent, from factory arms
  to chatbots, all inheriting the forced-labor etymology
- "Robotic process automation" (RPA) -- software that performs repetitive
  digital tasks, explicitly framed as a digital worker doing the drudgery
- "Bot" -- truncation that has lost even the memory of "robot," used for
  software agents from chatbots to web crawlers
- "The robots are coming for our jobs" -- the labor-replacement anxiety
  that the metaphor's servitude frame makes inevitable
- "Robot uprising" -- the Capek rebellion narrative applied to AI,
  military drones, or any autonomous technology
- "Do you want Skynet? Because that's how you get Skynet" -- the rebellion
  narrative compressed into a cultural catchphrase

## Origin Story

Karel Capek premiered *R.U.R.* in Prague in January 1921. The play was an
immediate international sensation, translated into thirty languages within
three years. Capek himself credited his brother Josef with suggesting the
word "robot" -- Karel had originally planned to call his creations "labori"
(from Latin "labor"), but Josef proposed the Czech word with its stronger
connotations of serfdom and compulsory service. The play's plot -- robots
are manufactured as cheap labor, replace human workers, develop
consciousness, rebel, and ultimately inherit the earth -- established the
template that science fiction has been reworking for a century. By the 1940s,
Asimov was already writing against the rebellion narrative with his Three
Laws of Robotics, but the word itself continued to carry the servitude
frame. Today "robot" is so deeply embedded in everyday language that its
metaphorical origin has disappeared entirely: people use it to describe
vacuum cleaners, stock-trading algorithms, and social media accounts without
any awareness that they are invoking a 1920 Czech play about the moral
consequences of manufactured servitude.
