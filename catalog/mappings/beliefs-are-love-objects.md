## What It Brings

You can fall in love with an idea. This metaphor maps the emotional
structure of romantic love -- attraction, infatuation, devotion,
heartbreak -- onto the domain of belief, so that holding a conviction
becomes a love affair and losing it becomes a breakup. The mapping
imports the full affective arc of romantic attachment into intellectual
life.

Key structural parallels:

- **Attraction and infatuation** -- "He fell in love with Marxism in
  college." "She was seduced by the elegance of the theory." "I'm
  attracted to that idea." Initial encounter with a belief has the
  phenomenology of romantic attraction: something about it draws you
  in, captivates you, won't let you go. The metaphor explains why
  new converts are so passionate -- they are in the infatuation stage.
- **Devotion and loyalty** -- "She's wedded to that theory." "He's
  devoted to the cause." "They're true believers." Long-term belief
  is modeled on long-term commitment: you stay faithful to an idea,
  you don't stray, you defend it against rivals. The metaphor makes
  intellectual consistency look like relational fidelity.
- **Jealousy and exclusivity** -- "He can't serve two masters." "You
  can't love two theories equally." The romantic frame imports
  exclusivity into belief: true commitment to one belief system means
  not being committed to its rivals. Intellectual promiscuity -- the
  willingness to entertain multiple frameworks -- becomes a kind of
  faithlessness.
- **Heartbreak and disillusionment** -- "He was disillusioned with
  socialism." "She had a painful break with the church." "The theory
  he loved turned out to be false." Losing a belief is not just a
  cognitive adjustment; it is a breakup, with all the attendant grief,
  anger, and sense of betrayal. The metaphor captures the emotional
  devastation that can accompany a change of worldview.
- **The beloved's flaws** -- "Love is blind -- and so is ideology."
  "He couldn't see the theory's flaws because he was too attached to
  it." The romantic frame explains confirmation bias: when you love
  something, you overlook its faults. The metaphor suggests that
  intellectual attachment distorts perception in exactly the way
  romantic attachment does.

## Where It Breaks

- **Beliefs are not agents** -- a love object is (typically) another
  person, an agent with independent existence, feelings, and agency.
  A belief has no feelings about being believed. The metaphor can
  create a false sense of reciprocity, as though your devotion to
  an idea earns you something in return, when in fact the relationship
  is entirely one-directional.
- **The metaphor pathologizes revision** -- in romance, changing
  partners is a breakup, a failure. The love-object metaphor maps
  this onto belief change, making the rational revision of beliefs
  in light of new evidence feel like infidelity or abandonment. This
  can make people cling to discredited beliefs out of a sense of
  loyalty that has no epistemic warrant.
- **Infatuation is not insight** -- the metaphor gives the initial
  excitement of encountering a new idea the structure of falling in
  love, which feels like a revelation. But romantic infatuation is
  notoriously unreliable as a guide to long-term compatibility, and
  intellectual infatuation is similarly unreliable as a guide to
  truth. The metaphor normalizes the conflation of excitement with
  validity.
- **The metaphor cannot represent collaborative inquiry** -- love
  is typically dyadic (lover and beloved), but intellectual life
  at its best is communal. The love-object metaphor has no natural
  way to represent a community of inquirers collectively refining
  a belief; it keeps returning to the individual devotee and the
  singular beloved idea.

## Expressions

- "He fell in love with that theory" -- initial intellectual
  attraction as romantic infatuation
- "She's wedded to that ideology" -- deep commitment as marriage
- "He was seduced by the argument" -- persuasion as romantic
  seduction
- "She had a bitter divorce from the party" -- leaving a belief
  system as a painful breakup
- "He's flirting with libertarianism" -- tentative engagement
  as romantic flirtation
- "She's faithful to her principles" -- consistency as romantic
  fidelity
- "He was betrayed by a theory he trusted" -- disconfirmation as
  romantic betrayal
- "They embraced the new doctrine" -- adoption of a belief as
  a physical embrace
- "She can't let go of that idea" -- persistent belief as
  inability to end a relationship

## Origin Story

BELIEFS ARE LOVE OBJECTS appears in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff,
Espenson & Schwartz, 1991) as one of the more emotionally charged
metaphors in the belief cluster. Where BELIEFS ARE POSSESSIONS treats
convictions as inert objects you hold and BELIEFS ARE GUIDES treats
them as agents that lead you, BELIEFS ARE LOVE OBJECTS treats them
as agents you are emotionally bound to. The metaphor draws on the
full structure of the romantic relationship frame: attraction, courtship,
commitment, jealousy, and heartbreak.

The metaphor is especially visible in accounts of ideological conversion
and deconversion, where the emotional trajectory of belief change mirrors
the arc of a love affair. Religious conversion narratives often use
romantic language explicitly ("I gave my heart to Christ"), and accounts
of political disillusionment frequently reach for breakup vocabulary
("I had to leave the movement I loved").
