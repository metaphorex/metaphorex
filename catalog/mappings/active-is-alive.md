## What It Brings

Things that are active are alive; things that are inactive are dead. This
metaphor maps the fundamental biological distinction between living and
non-living onto the distinction between active and inactive states. A
functioning process is "alive," a halted one is "dead," and restarting
something is "bringing it back to life." The metaphor is so pervasive
that it operates below conscious awareness in everyday language across
many domains -- from technology to markets to conversations.

Key structural parallels:

- **Activity as life** -- "The project is alive and well." "Keep the
  conversation alive." "The market is lively today." When something is
  functioning, changing, responding, or producing effects, it is
  conceptualized as a living thing. Activity is the defining property of
  life, so anything that exhibits activity inherits the quality of
  aliveness.

- **Inactivity as death** -- "The project is dead." "That proposal is
  dead on arrival." "The phone went dead." When activity ceases, the
  entity is dead. The mapping is absolute: there is no "sleeping" or
  "resting" intermediate state in most expressions. The binary of
  alive/dead maps onto the binary of active/inactive.

- **Resumption as resurrection** -- "She revived the project." "They
  resurrected the old plan." "The brand came back to life." When an
  inactive thing becomes active again, the metaphor frames it as
  returning from the dead. This gives resumption a dramatic, almost
  miraculous quality.

- **Gradual decline as dying** -- "The initiative is dying." "Support
  for the bill is fading." "The movement is on life support." When
  activity diminishes gradually, the entity is dying -- losing life force
  over time. This maps the trajectory of biological decline onto the
  trajectory of decreasing activity.

- **Vitality as productive capacity** -- "It's a vibrant community."
  "The economy is healthy." "The ecosystem is thriving." Active things
  that produce abundant effects are not merely alive but flourishing.
  The degree of activity maps onto degrees of biological vitality.

## Where It Breaks

- **The binary is too stark** -- biological life admits gradations
  (dormancy, hibernation, coma, vegetation), but the metaphor typically
  deploys only the alive/dead binary. A project that is paused, a market
  that is quiet, or a tradition that is practiced rarely -- these are
  treated as either alive or dead, with little vocabulary for the states
  in between. The metaphor forces a judgment where ambiguity might be
  more accurate.

- **The metaphor implies organic autonomy** -- living things are
  self-sustaining; they generate their own activity. But many active
  systems (organizations, technologies, traditions) require constant
  external input to remain active. Calling them "alive" obscures the
  labor and resources keeping them going. When a project "dies," the
  metaphor hides who stopped feeding it.

- **Death implies permanence** -- in biology, death is (with rare
  exceptions) irreversible. But "dead" projects, markets, and ideas
  frequently return. The metaphor overcommits to finality, which is why
  "resurrection" language is needed as a corrective -- and resurrection
  itself carries connotations of the miraculous that distort how ordinary
  reactivation actually is.

- **The metaphor personifies systems inappropriately** -- calling a
  process "alive" invites attributing intentions, feelings, and needs to
  it. A "dying" organization evokes sympathy; a "thriving" one evokes
  admiration. These emotional responses, imported from our relationship
  to living beings, can distort decision-making about systems that have
  no subjective experience.

- **Voluntary inactivity becomes death** -- choosing to pause, to go
  fallow, to deliberately reduce activity -- these strategic choices
  look like dying in this metaphor. Agricultural fields that lie fallow
  are not dead; they are regenerating. But the metaphor has no way to
  frame chosen inactivity as healthy.

## Expressions

- "The project is alive and well" -- ongoing activity as healthy life
- "That idea is dead" -- an inactive concept as a lifeless thing
- "Bring the discussion back to life" -- reactivating as resurrection
- "The market is dead today" -- low activity as lifelessness
- "It's a lively debate" -- energetic activity as vigorous life
- "She breathed new life into the department" -- reactivation as
  life-giving
- "The phone went dead" -- a device ceasing function as dying
- "Dead on arrival" -- something that was never active as stillborn
- "The movement is on life support" -- barely active as artificially
  sustained life
- "Keep it alive" -- maintaining activity as sustaining life
- "A living tradition" -- a continuing practice as a living organism

## Origin Story

ACTIVE IS ALIVE appears in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson &
Schwartz, 1991) and reflects one of the most basic conceptual mappings in
human cognition. The link between activity and life is grounded in the
most primal distinction a human perceiver makes: does this thing move on
its own? If yes, it is probably alive and demands attention (it might be
prey, predator, or conspecific). If no, it is probably inert and can be
ignored. This perceptual heuristic -- motion detection as life detection
-- becomes a conceptual metaphor that extends far beyond the biological:
anything that exhibits activity, change, or responsiveness inherits the
cognitive status of a living thing.

The metaphor interacts with PEOPLE ARE MACHINES (which maps in the
opposite direction, treating living agents as mechanical) and with the
Event Structure system (where activity is motion). It is arguably more
primitive than either of these: the life/death distinction may be the
earliest categorical judgment available to human cognition.
