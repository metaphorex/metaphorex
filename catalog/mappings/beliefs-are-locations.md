## What It Brings

A belief is a place where you stand. This metaphor maps spatial location
onto the domain of belief, so that holding a conviction becomes occupying
a position, changing your mind becomes moving to a new place, and
agreeing with someone becomes standing in the same spot they do. The
mapping imports the full spatial logic of location -- proximity, distance,
boundaries, terrain -- into intellectual life.

Key structural parallels:

- **Beliefs as positions** -- "Where do you stand on this issue?" "She
  holds a position on the left." "He's moved to a more moderate stance."
  A belief is a point in an abstract space, and to believe something is
  to occupy that point. The spatial metaphor turns the landscape of
  possible beliefs into a map that can be navigated.
- **Agreement as co-location** -- "We're on the same page." "They're
  miles apart on this." "She's come around to my position." When two
  people share a belief, they stand in the same place; when they
  disagree, there is distance between them. This spatial rendering
  of agreement makes intellectual affinity feel like physical
  proximity.
- **Commitment as immobility** -- "He won't budge on this." "She's
  firmly rooted in her convictions." "Nothing can move him." To be
  strongly committed to a belief is to be fixed in place, immovable.
  The metaphor makes intellectual stubbornness feel like physical
  solidity and changing one's mind feel like being dislodged.
- **Belief change as movement** -- "I've shifted my position." "She
  drifted toward conservatism." "He's come a long way on this issue."
  Changing a belief is traveling from one location to another. The
  metaphor preserves directionality (you can move left or right,
  forward or backward) and gradualness (you can drift, shift, or
  leap).
- **Cognitive territory** -- "He's in uncharted territory with this
  theory." "That argument is on solid ground." "She's out on a limb."
  Beliefs exist in a landscape that has terrain features -- solid
  ground, thin ice, high ground, swamps -- and these features represent
  the epistemic quality of the belief.

## Where It Breaks

- **Beliefs are not positions on a single axis** -- political beliefs
  are routinely plotted on a left-right axis, but most belief systems
  are multidimensional. The spatial metaphor, especially in its
  one-dimensional "spectrum" form, flattens complex ideological
  structures into a line. Two people can be at the "same position"
  on one axis and radically opposed on another.
- **Standing still is not the same as certainty** -- the metaphor
  equates conviction with immobility ("He won't move on this"), which
  makes certainty look like rigidity. A person can be deeply certain
  of a belief while remaining open to revising it; the spatial metaphor
  has no way to represent confident flexibility.
- **The metaphor makes belief change feel like dislocation** -- to
  change your mind is to leave your position, which triggers the
  disorientation associated with spatial displacement. This may
  explain why changing one's mind feels psychologically costly even
  when the new belief is better supported: the metaphor makes it
  feel like losing your footing.
- **Co-location is not real agreement** -- the metaphor says that
  people who "stand in the same place" agree. But two people can
  occupy the same nominal position for entirely different reasons,
  and their apparent agreement may conceal deep disagreement about
  foundations. The spatial metaphor confuses positional overlap with
  genuine intellectual alignment.

## Expressions

- "Where do you stand on this?" -- belief as spatial position
- "He won't budge on this issue" -- commitment as immobility
- "She's shifted her position" -- belief change as spatial movement
- "They're miles apart" -- disagreement as spatial distance
- "I've come around to your view" -- agreement as arriving at
  someone's location
- "He's on solid ground with that argument" -- well-supported belief
  as stable terrain
- "She's out on a limb" -- a risky or isolated belief as a precarious
  position
- "We need to find common ground" -- seeking agreement as seeking
  shared territory
- "He's drifted to the right" -- gradual belief change as lateral
  movement
- "That position is indefensible" -- a weak belief as an exposed
  military position

## Origin Story

BELIEFS ARE LOCATIONS is documented in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff,
Espenson & Schwartz, 1991) as one of several metaphors that structure the
domain of belief. It derives from the more general STATES ARE LOCATIONS
mapping in the event structure metaphor system: if states in general are
locations, then beliefs -- as a particular kind of cognitive state -- are
a particular kind of location. The political vocabulary of "positions,"
"stances," and "where you stand" makes this mapping especially visible.

The metaphor's spatial structure has been inherited by the language of
academic and political discourse. "The political spectrum," "the
Overton window," "shifting the goalposts" -- all presuppose a spatial
landscape of beliefs that can be mapped, measured, and navigated.
