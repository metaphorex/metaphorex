## What It Brings

In *Novum Organum* (1620), Book I, Aphorisms XXXIX-XLIV, Francis Bacon
identifies four classes of systematic error that obstruct human
understanding. He calls them "Idols" -- *idola* -- false images that the
mind worships in place of truth. The Idols of the Tribe (*Idola Tribus*)
are distortions inherent in human nature itself. They afflict everyone,
not because of individual temperament or cultural circumstance, but
because of the structure of the human mind.

- **The mind as a false mirror** -- Bacon's central image for the Tribe
  idols is the mind as "an uneven mirror" that "distorts and discolours
  the nature of things by mingling its own nature with it." The metaphor
  maps the optical properties of a warped reflective surface onto the
  cognitive properties of the human intellect. Just as a curved mirror
  produces a systematic distortion (not random noise, but a predictable
  pattern of stretching and compression), the human mind produces
  systematic errors. This structure anticipates what cognitive psychology
  would later formalize as cognitive biases: not random mistakes, but
  reliable, species-wide patterns of distortion.

- **The tribe is the human species** -- Bacon's "tribe" is not a
  particular culture or society but humanity as a whole. The Idols of
  the Tribe are species-level errors, built into the cognitive equipment
  that every human shares. The metaphor maps the sociological concept of
  tribal identity (shared characteristics that define group membership)
  onto the biological concept of species-wide cognitive architecture.
  This is a remarkably modern move: Bacon is claiming that the source of
  error is not ignorance (which can be remedied by education) or
  deception (which can be remedied by honesty) but the structure of
  human cognition itself (which can only be compensated for, never
  eliminated).

- **Idols demand worship; biases operate unconsciously** -- by calling
  cognitive distortions "idols," Bacon maps the religious structure of
  false worship onto the epistemological structure of systematic error.
  An idol is not a trivial mistake; it is an object of devotion. The
  mind does not merely err toward confirmation bias -- it is drawn to
  confirming evidence the way a worshipper is drawn to a shrine. The
  metaphor imports the compulsive, unreflective quality of idolatry:
  you do not choose to worship an idol; you are captured by it. This
  maps onto the involuntary nature of cognitive biases -- knowing about
  the anchoring effect does not immunize you against it.

- **Specific Tribe errors Bacon identifies** -- the tendency to perceive
  more order than exists (pattern-matching bias), the tendency to give
  more weight to confirming than disconfirming evidence (confirmation
  bias), the tendency to be swayed by sudden or striking impressions
  rather than gradual evidence (availability heuristic), and the
  tendency of the senses to fail at extremes (perceptual limitations).
  Bacon anticipated the core catalog of cognitive biases 350 years
  before Kahneman and Tversky.

## Where It Breaks

- **Idols imply a true religion; biases do not imply perfect cognition**
  -- the metaphor of idolatry only works against a background assumption
  that there is a true god to worship instead. In Bacon's framework, the
  "true god" is nature as it actually is, accessible through the correct
  inductive method. But cognitive science has complicated this: there may
  be no view from nowhere, no unbiased cognition to achieve. If all
  perception is theory-laden and all reasoning is situated, then
  "smashing the idols" does not reveal unmediated truth. The metaphor
  implies a correctable deviation from a baseline of accuracy, but that
  baseline may not exist.

- **The religious framing moralizes cognitive error** -- calling biases
  "idols" makes them sound like sins. Idolatry is a moral failure in
  Abrahamic religion: the worshipper has a duty to recognize the true
  god and is culpable for failing to do so. When Bacon maps this onto
  cognitive bias, he imports a structure of blame. But cognitive biases
  are not moral failures -- they are often adaptive heuristics that
  served evolutionary purposes. The pattern-matching bias that Bacon
  condemns as an idol is the same capacity that allows rapid threat
  detection. The metaphor makes it difficult to see biases as trade-offs
  rather than errors.

- **The tribe metaphor suggests universality that may not hold** --
  Bacon claims these distortions are universal to the human species.
  But cross-cultural psychology (Henrich et al., "The WEIRDest People
  in the World") has shown that many "universal" cognitive patterns are
  actually WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic)
  artifacts. Some of Bacon's "Tribe" idols may be more tribal than he
  intended -- characteristic of particular epistemic cultures rather
  than of Homo sapiens as a whole.

- **Bacon offers no mechanism for detecting one's own idols** -- if the
  idols are built into the mind's architecture, how does one recognize
  them from inside? Bacon's answer (the inductive method) is circular:
  you need unbiased cognition to apply the method correctly, but you
  need the method to correct your biases. The metaphor of the false
  mirror does not explain who holds up the true mirror for comparison.

## Expressions

- "Idols of the Tribe" -- used in philosophy of science and critical
  thinking to refer to species-wide cognitive biases, directly from
  Bacon's terminology

- "Tribal thinking" -- the generalized form, now more commonly
  associated with political polarization and in-group bias than with
  Bacon's original species-level meaning, showing how the metaphor
  has drifted from universal to factional

- "The mind is not a clean mirror" -- a paraphrase of Bacon's central
  image, used in epistemology and cognitive science to argue that
  observation is always theory-laden

- "Cognitive idol" -- occasional usage in rationalist communities
  (LessWrong, Effective Altruism discourse) to describe systematically
  worshipped bad reasoning patterns, explicitly echoing Bacon

## Origin Story

Bacon's four Idols are among the earliest systematic attempts to catalog
the sources of human error in reasoning. The word *idola* deliberately
echoes both the biblical prohibition on graven images and the
Epicurean concept of *eidola* (thin films shed by objects that enter the
senses and can produce false impressions). Bacon synthesized the
religious and philosophical traditions into a single framework: the
mind is beset by false images that it mistakes for reality.

The Idols of the Tribe were the most philosophically ambitious of the
four categories because they made a claim about human nature itself --
not about individual quirks, social conventions, or intellectual
fashions, but about the species. This claim would not be rigorously
tested until the cognitive revolution of the 1960s-1970s, when
Kahneman, Tversky, and others began experimentally documenting the
systematic biases Bacon had described metaphorically. The metaphor
preceded the science by three and a half centuries.
