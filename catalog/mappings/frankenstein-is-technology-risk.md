## What It Brings

In Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* (1818), Victor
Frankenstein assembles a creature from dead body parts and brings it to life
through an unspecified scientific process. The creature is intelligent,
articulate, and initially benevolent -- but Frankenstein is horrified by what
he has made and abandons it. Rejected by its creator and by every human it
encounters, the creature turns violent and ultimately destroys everything
Frankenstein loves. The metaphor maps this structure -- a creator who brings
something into existence, loses control of it, and is destroyed by it --
onto any technology, institution, or system that turns against its maker's
intentions.

- **The creation is not evil; it is abandoned** -- this is the structural
  element most people miss. Shelley's creature is not inherently monstrous.
  It learns language, reads Milton, and craves companionship. It becomes
  violent only after systematic rejection. The metaphor, when used with
  fidelity to the source, imports the structure of creator responsibility:
  the problem is not that the thing was made, but that the maker refused
  to care for it after making it. This maps onto technologies released
  without safety frameworks, products launched without support
  infrastructure, and AI systems deployed without monitoring -- the
  Frankenstein problem is not creation but abandonment.
- **The creator is repelled by his own creation** -- Frankenstein's first
  response to the living creature is revulsion. He has spent years working
  toward this moment, and when it arrives, he runs. The metaphor captures
  a pattern visible in technology development: the innovator who is so
  focused on whether something can be done that they do not prepare for
  the reality of what it looks like when it works. The first successful
  cloning, the first deepfake, the first autonomous weapon -- each
  produced a version of Frankenstein's recoil.
- **The creature exceeds its creator's understanding** -- Frankenstein
  cannot explain how the creature works, cannot predict its behavior, and
  cannot control it. The metaphor imports the structure of opacity: the
  thing you made is now beyond your comprehension. This maps onto machine
  learning systems whose decision processes are opaque to their
  developers, genetic modifications whose downstream effects are
  unpredictable, and any complex system that has passed beyond its
  creator's ability to fully understand or govern it.
- **The name transfers from creator to creation** -- in popular usage,
  "Frankenstein" refers to the monster, not the doctor. This linguistic
  drift is itself metaphorically significant: the creator becomes
  indistinguishable from the creation in public memory. The thing that
  was made subsumes the identity of the one who made it. This maps onto
  how technologies consume their creators' legacies: Oppenheimer is
  inseparable from the bomb, Nobel from dynamite, Zuckerberg from the
  platform's consequences.

## Where It Breaks

- **Shelley's creature is singular; real technology risks are systemic** --
  Frankenstein makes one creature. The horror is personal and contained:
  one monster, one creator, a finite number of victims. But real
  technology risks are distributed and systemic. Climate change is not
  a monster; it is an emergent property of billions of individual
  decisions. AI risk is not one rogue system; it is an ecosystem of
  interacting systems with no single creator. The Frankenstein metaphor
  personalizes and singularizes risks that are actually diffuse,
  collaborative, and structural. This makes the problem feel more
  tractable than it is (just kill the monster) and assigns blame more
  neatly than reality allows (just punish the creator).
- **The metaphor frames all creation as hubris** -- "playing God" and
  "Frankenstein" are deployed as warnings against innovation itself. But
  Shelley's actual moral is more nuanced: the problem is not that
  Frankenstein created life but that he did so irresponsibly and then
  abandoned his creation. The popular version of the metaphor collapses
  this distinction, treating the act of creation as inherently
  transgressive. This can produce a reflexive technophobia that opposes
  all novel technologies -- genetic medicine, nuclear energy, artificial
  intelligence -- under the same Frankensteinian banner, without
  distinguishing between responsible and irresponsible development.
- **The creature is conscious and suffering; most technologies are not** --
  the emotional power of Shelley's novel comes from the creature's
  interiority: it feels pain, loneliness, and rage. When we call an AI
  system "Frankenstein's monster," we implicitly attribute consciousness
  and suffering to it, which may be profoundly misleading. A language
  model that produces harmful outputs is not suffering from rejection.
  A self-driving car that kills a pedestrian is not enraged. The metaphor
  anthropomorphizes technological failures, making them seem like
  betrayals (the creation turned on us) rather than engineering failures
  (the system did not work as specified).
- **"Frankenstein" has become shorthand for ugliness and patchwork** --
  in common usage, "Frankenstein" often means "cobbled together from
  mismatched parts" (a "Frankenstein bill" in politics, a "Frankenstein
  system" in software). This usage has drifted far from Shelley's
  concerns about creator responsibility and landed on a purely aesthetic
  complaint about incoherence. The metaphor now carries two contradictory
  meanings -- "dangerous creation that turns on its maker" and "ugly
  thing assembled from incompatible parts" -- and speakers often conflate
  them.
- **The novel is a product of its era's anxieties, not ours** -- Shelley
  wrote during the early Industrial Revolution, when galvanism (electrical
  stimulation of dead tissue) was the cutting-edge technology that seemed
  to blur the line between life and death. The anxieties the novel
  encodes -- about vitalism, about the soul, about whether science could
  literally reanimate the dead -- are not the same anxieties that drive
  modern technology risk discourse. Applying "Frankenstein" to AI or
  genetic engineering borrows the emotional register of early 19th-century
  horror and applies it to 21st-century problems with very different
  structural features.

## Expressions

- "Frankenstein's monster" -- the standard reference for any creation
  that turns against its creator, so common that the distinction between
  creator (Frankenstein) and creation (the monster) is routinely lost
- "Franken-" as prefix -- a productive morpheme attached to anything
  perceived as an unnatural hybrid: "Frankenfood" (genetically modified
  organisms), "Frankenbill" (legislation assembled from incompatible
  provisions), "Frankenstein code" (software stitched together from
  mismatched components)
- "Playing God" -- the broader frame that Frankenstein exemplifies,
  applied to genetic engineering, cloning, synthetic biology, and any
  technology that creates or modifies life
- "The monster always turns on its creator" -- the folk-wisdom
  distillation of the Frankenstein narrative, used as a cautionary
  principle in technology ethics discussions
- "It's alive!" -- from the 1931 James Whale film rather than the novel,
  this exclamation has become the generic expression of horror at a
  creation that has exceeded expectations or control, used ironically
  in software development when a system unexpectedly starts working

## Origin Story

Mary Shelley conceived *Frankenstein* during the summer of 1816 at the
Villa Diodati near Lake Geneva, where she, Percy Bysshe Shelley, Lord
Byron, and John Polidori challenged each other to write ghost stories
during a period of unusually cold, dark weather caused by the eruption
of Mount Tambora. Shelley was eighteen years old. She later described
the origin as a waking dream: "I saw the pale student of unhallowed arts
kneeling beside the thing he had put together."

The novel was published anonymously in 1818 and attributed to Mary Shelley
in the 1831 revised edition. Its subtitle, "The Modern Prometheus," links
Frankenstein explicitly to the mythological figure who stole fire from the
gods and was punished for it -- framing scientific transgression as a
variation on an ancient pattern.

The 1931 Universal Studios film, starring Boris Karloff as the creature,
transformed public understanding of the story: the creature became
inarticulate and violent (rather than eloquent and tragic), the creation
scene involved visible lightning and machinery (rather than Shelley's
deliberate vagueness), and the creature was given bolts in its neck that
appear nowhere in the novel. Most popular uses of the "Frankenstein"
metaphor draw on the film rather than the book, which means the metaphor
as commonly deployed is substantially simpler than the metaphor Shelley
actually wrote.

  1831) -- the source text, a novel about creator responsibility as much
