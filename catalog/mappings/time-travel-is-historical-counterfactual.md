## Transfers

Time travel stories are counterfactual history made literal. When a character
goes back to prevent an assassination or undo a mistake, they are performing
the same operation a historian does when asking "What if Napoleon had won at
Waterloo?" -- except the sci-fi version lets you see the result. The genre
externalizes a cognitive operation (imagining alternative pasts) into a
narrative device (a machine that takes you there).

Key structural parallels:

- **The decision point as destination** -- time travelers go to a specific
  moment, just as counterfactual historians isolate a specific juncture.
  Both frame history as a sequence of branching points where things could
  have gone differently. The metaphor makes historical contingency feel
  navigable.
- **Causal chains as timelines** -- change one event and the future
  reorganizes itself. *Back to the Future*'s fading photograph is a
  visualization of counterfactual reasoning: if you remove a cause, the
  effects disappear. This makes causation feel mechanical and linear.
- **The butterfly effect** -- Ray Bradbury's "A Sound of Thunder" (1952)
  gave counterfactual sensitivity analysis its most famous image: step
  on a butterfly in the Cretaceous and the present changes completely.
  The story is a thought experiment about causal sensitivity disguised
  as adventure fiction.
- **The observer's paradox** -- time travelers change history by
  participating in it, paralleling the problem that counterfactual
  reasoning always imports the reasoner's present-day knowledge and
  values into the hypothetical past.

## Limits

- **Time travel assumes clean branching; history is overdetermined** --
  the genre needs a single pivot point to build a plot around. Real
  historical events have hundreds of contributing causes, and removing
  one rarely produces a clean alternative timeline. The metaphor makes
  history feel more contingent than it may actually be.
- **Counterfactuals are not experiments** -- time travel narratives let
  you run the experiment: go back, change one variable, observe the
  result. Real counterfactual reasoning has no feedback loop. You can
  never know what would have actually happened, only what you can argue
  might have happened. The metaphor imports false empiricism into
  speculative reasoning.
- **The metaphor privileges individual agency** -- time travel plots
  almost always focus on a single person making a single choice. This
  imports a "great man" theory of history into counterfactual reasoning,
  obscuring structural causes (economic systems, demographics, geography)
  that no individual intervention could alter.
- **Paradox reveals the metaphor's limits** -- the grandfather paradox
  (you cannot go back and prevent your own existence) has no analogue in
  counterfactual history because counterfactual reasoning does not
  require self-consistency. The paradox is an artifact of literalizing
  the metaphor, not a feature of the cognitive operation it represents.

## Expressions

- "What if we could go back and change it?" -- the time-travel framing
  of counterfactual regret, common in political and personal discourse
- "You can't change the past" -- the anti-time-travel truism, often
  deployed to shut down counterfactual reasoning
- "The butterfly effect" -- from Bradbury via chaos theory, now standard
  shorthand for sensitive dependence on initial conditions
- "Sliding doors moment" -- from the 1998 film, used for any life event
  where a small difference would have produced a radically different outcome
- "History would have been different if..." -- the canonical counterfactual
  opener, structurally identical to a time-travel premise
- "We're living in the darkest timeline" -- from *Community* (2012), now
  common internet parlance for the feeling that history went wrong at some
  branching point

## Origin Story

H.G. Wells's *The Time Machine* (1895) established the genre, but the
counterfactual mapping became explicit with Bradbury's "A Sound of Thunder"
(1952), which turned time travel into a parable about historical causation.
Academic historians formalized counterfactual reasoning in the 1990s --
Niall Ferguson's *Virtual History* (1997) argued that counterfactuals are
essential to causal claims -- but the public already understood the concept
through decades of sci-fi. The genre had done the philosophical work of
making counterfactual reasoning intuitive by giving it characters, stakes,
and special effects.
