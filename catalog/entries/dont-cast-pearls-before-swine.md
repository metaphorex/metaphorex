## Transfers

The proverb originates in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 7:6):
"Give not that which is holy unto the dogs, neither cast ye your
pearls before swine, lest they trample them under their feet, and
turn again and rend you." The agricultural frame is specific: a
farmer scattering pearls in a pig pen. The pigs cannot eat pearls,
cannot recognize their value, and will respond with the only
behaviors available to them -- rooting, trampling, and aggression
toward the confusing stimulus. The waste is total: the pearls are
destroyed, and the giver is attacked.

Key structural parallels:

- **Value requires a capacity for recognition.** Pearls are valuable
  only to creatures that can perceive their properties -- luster,
  rarity, beauty. To a pig, a pearl is a hard, inedible object
  indistinguishable from a stone. The metaphor encodes a theory of
  value as relational rather than intrinsic: nothing is universally
  precious. An advanced technical explanation offered to a non-technical
  audience, a subtle literary allusion in a mass-market advertisement,
  a sophisticated investment opportunity presented to someone without
  financial literacy -- each is pearls before swine. The value is
  real but the audience cannot access it.

- **Mismatched generosity produces hostility.** The proverb does not
  merely warn of waste. It warns of danger: the swine "turn again
  and rend you." Offering something valuable to someone who cannot
  recognize its value does not produce gratitude or even
  indifference -- it produces resentment. The recipient perceives
  the offering as confusing, condescending, or threatening. A
  manager who shares complex strategic analysis with a team that
  lacks context does not get appreciation; they get suspicion about
  hidden agendas. A whistleblower who presents evidence of
  corruption to the corrupt institution does not get reform; they
  get retaliation.

- **The giver bears responsibility for audience selection.** The
  imperative is directed at the pearl-holder, not the swine. The
  proverb does not say "swine should learn to appreciate pearls" --
  it says "you should not cast pearls before swine." The structural
  transfer is that the communicator, not the audience, is
  responsible for matching the offering to the recipient. This is a
  theory of rhetorical responsibility: know your audience before
  you commit your resources.

- **Scarcity makes audience selection a resource allocation problem.**
  Pearls are rare. Every pearl cast before swine is a pearl not given
  to someone who would value it. The metaphor imports an opportunity
  cost: misallocated generosity is not just waste but theft from the
  audience that would have benefited. Time spent explaining complex
  ideas to people who do not care is time not spent explaining them
  to people who would act on them.

## Limits

- **It naturalizes the audience's incapacity.** A pig cannot learn to
  value pearls -- the limitation is biological and permanent. But
  human audiences can learn, grow, and change their evaluative
  frameworks. Treating an audience as permanently swine-like forecloses
  education, persuasion, and bridge-building. Many "pearls before
  swine" diagnoses are actually failures of translation: the offering
  was valuable but the presentation was wrong. The metaphor blames
  the audience for what may be the giver's failure to communicate.

- **It flatters the giver.** The metaphor positions the speaker as the
  holder of pearls and the audience as swine. This is an extremely
  comfortable self-assessment. In practice, much of what people
  consider their "pearls" -- their expertise, opinions, insights --
  is less rare and less valuable than they believe. The proverb
  provides a ready-made excuse for failed communication: "They didn't
  appreciate my brilliance" is more flattering than "My idea wasn't
  as good as I thought" or "I explained it badly."

- **It justifies gatekeeping.** If valuable knowledge should not be
  shared with those who cannot appreciate it, the proverb provides
  a principled basis for restricting access. Historically, it has
  been used to justify withholding education from the poor, excluding
  laypeople from professional knowledge, and maintaining elite
  control of information. The metaphor's structure -- precious things
  belong with those who recognize their value -- aligns with every
  form of intellectual snobbery.

- **It assumes the pearls are authentic.** The proverb takes for
  granted that what the giver holds is genuinely valuable. But
  self-described "pearls" may be conventional wisdom, recycled
  platitudes, or narrow expertise that the speaker has inflated. The
  "swine" may be exercising legitimate judgment in rejecting
  offerings that are not, in fact, pearls. The metaphor provides no
  structural test for pearl-authenticity, only for audience-capacity.

## Expressions

- "Don't cast your pearls before swine" -- the standard form,
  advising against sharing valuable things with those who cannot
  appreciate them
- "Pearls before swine" -- the compressed form, used as a noun
  phrase to describe any instance of wasted sophistication
- "Casting pearls" -- verbal form meaning to share expertise or
  resources with an unreceptive audience
- "That's pearls before swine" -- evaluative form, diagnosing a
  communication mismatch after the fact
- "Pearls Before Swine" -- Stephan Pastis's comic strip (2001-present),
  whose title plays on the proverb's implication that the audience
  may not deserve what it receives

## Origin Story

The proverb's source is the Sermon on the Mount, specifically
Matthew 7:6, where it appears in the context of moral instruction
about discrimination in sharing sacred teachings. The agricultural
imagery would have been immediately legible to first-century
Palestinian audiences, for whom pigs were both ritually unclean
and economically familiar.

The phrase entered English through the Vulgate and the Wycliffe
Bible (1382), and was thoroughly established by the time of the
King James Version (1611). Its application broadened rapidly from
religious instruction to general advice about audience selection.
By the eighteenth century it was a standard figure of speech with
no necessary religious connotation.

The metaphor is now dead in the technical sense: most speakers who
use "pearls before swine" are not consciously invoking a farmyard
scene. The pearl-as-value and swine-as-incapacity mappings have
become so conventional that the phrase functions as a single lexical
unit rather than an active comparison. The corruption to "casting
perils before swine" occasionally appears, revealing that even
"pearls" is fading from the active imagery.
