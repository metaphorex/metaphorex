## Transfers

Herbert Freudenberger borrowed the term "burn-out" in 1974 from the drug
culture slang for the effects of chronic substance use, but the deeper
metaphorical structure draws on fire. A person who has burned out is framed
as a combustible material that has been consumed: the fuel is gone, and what
remains is ash. The metaphor is so thoroughly dead that most speakers do not
consciously register the fire imagery, yet it shapes how burnout is understood,
treated, and -- critically -- blamed.

Key structural parallels:

- **Person as fuel** -- the most consequential mapping. The metaphor frames
  the worker as the combustible material, not the match or the arsonist. This
  locates the problem in the individual's capacity rather than in the system
  that ignited them. "She burned out" has the same grammatical structure as
  "the candle burned out" -- the subject is consumed by a process that appears
  to originate within itself. The organization that set the conditions for
  combustion is absent from the sentence.
- **Total consumption** -- a burned-out fire has not merely diminished; it has
  ceased. The metaphor implies a binary state: burning or extinguished. This
  maps onto the clinical observation that burnout is not just tiredness but a
  qualitative transformation -- the person who burned out cannot simply rest
  and resume. Something structural has changed. Maslach's three dimensions
  (exhaustion, cynicism, reduced efficacy) describe a person who has not just
  run low but has been fundamentally altered.
- **Residue, not restoration** -- after a fire burns out, what remains is ash
  and char. The metaphor implies that burnout leaves damage, not a deficit.
  You do not "refuel" a burned-out fire; you rebuild from the residue. This
  maps onto the clinical reality that recovery from severe burnout often
  requires not just rest but reconstruction of one's relationship to work.
- **The fire triangle** -- fire requires heat, fuel, and oxygen. Remove any
  one and the fire extinguishes. The burnout metaphor implicitly invokes this
  structure: sustained demand (heat), personal resources (fuel), and
  organizational oxygen (autonomy, support, recognition). Maslach's later
  work on the "areas of worklife" model maps remarkably well onto the fire
  triangle -- burnout occurs when the conditions for healthy combustion
  (controlled, productive work) shift into conditions for destructive
  combustion (uncontrolled, consuming work).

## Limits

- **Recovery is possible; re-combustion of ash is not** -- the most
  significant structural failure. A log that has burned to ash cannot be
  reignited. A person who has burned out can, with sufficient change in
  conditions, recover. The metaphor's fatalism overstates the permanence of
  the damage and can discourage intervention -- if someone has "burned out,"
  the implication is that restoration is impossible, which is clinically
  false.
- **The fire has no agent** -- in the metaphor, burnout appears to happen
  spontaneously, as if the person simply combusted. This obscures the role
  of organizational conditions: excessive workload, insufficient autonomy,
  inadequate reward, unfairness, values conflict. Maslach was explicit that
  burnout is primarily a response to chronic workplace stressors, not a
  personal deficiency. The metaphor works against this understanding by
  making the person the grammatical subject of their own consumption.
- **Combustion is uniform; burnout is not** -- fire consumes fuel in a
  predictable chemical process. Burnout varies enormously between individuals
  in the same conditions. Personal history, coping strategies, meaning-making,
  and social support all modulate vulnerability. The metaphor implies a
  mechanical process that, once started, proceeds inevitably -- but human
  psychology is not thermodynamics.
- **The metaphor does not distinguish types of fire** -- a campfire, a house
  fire, and a forest fire are all "fire," but their dynamics, causes, and
  consequences differ profoundly. Similarly, burnout from overwork, burnout
  from moral injury, and burnout from boredom (bore-out) have different
  etiologies and require different interventions. The single word "burnout"
  collapses these distinctions.

## Expressions

- "She burned out" -- the canonical usage, framing the person as the consumed
  material
- "I'm running on fumes" -- approaching burnout, with the fuel nearly
  exhausted
- "Don't burn the candle at both ends" -- warning against accelerating
  consumption
- "He's just going through the motions -- the fire's gone out" -- describing
  the cynicism and disengagement that characterize advanced burnout
- "Burned out and burned up" -- intensifying the metaphor to suggest anger
  as well as exhaustion

## Origin Story

Herbert Freudenberger, a psychologist working in a free clinic in New York,
published "Staff Burn-Out" in the *Journal of Social Issues* in 1974. He
described the gradual emotional depletion he observed in idealistic young
volunteers who had entered human services work with high motivation. Christina
Maslach, working independently, developed the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI)
in 1981 and established the three-component model (emotional exhaustion,
depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment) that remains the
clinical standard.

The WHO included burnout in ICD-11 (2019) as an "occupational phenomenon"
rather than a medical condition, reflecting ongoing debate about whether
burnout is a disease, a syndrome, or a situational response. The fire
metaphor's implication that the person is the problem (fuel that ran out)
rather than the system (conditions that ignited uncontrolled combustion) has
been a persistent obstacle to organizational-level interventions.
