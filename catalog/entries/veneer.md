## Transfers

In woodworking, veneer is a thin slice of fine wood -- walnut, mahogany,
rosewood -- glued over a substrate of cheaper, more structurally stable
material like plywood or MDF. The technique is ancient (Egyptian
sarcophagi used ebony veneer over local wood) and in its original context
is neither cheap nor dishonest: it is a rational use of scarce decorative
material over abundant structural material.

The metaphorical transfer to social behavior inverts this evaluation.
"A veneer of civility," "a veneer of respectability," "a thin veneer of
competence" -- all treat the covering as deceptive and the substrate as
the damning truth. The structural parallels that survive the transfer:

- **Thinness as the defining property** -- veneer is measured in fractions
  of a millimeter. The metaphor foregrounds how little material separates
  the presented surface from the reality beneath. A "veneer of calm" is
  not deep composure; it is composure that could crack at any moment.
  The quantitative thinness of the source maps onto qualitative
  shallowness of the target.

- **Surface-substrate mismatch** -- the veneer and the substrate are
  different materials. The visible grain pattern belongs to one species
  of wood; the structural properties belong to another. This maps onto
  the claim that what someone displays (manners, credentials, polish)
  is categorically different from what they are (crude, unqualified,
  rough). The metaphor does not merely say the surface is exaggerated;
  it says the surface and the substance are different kinds of thing.

- **Grain-following as a constraint** -- a veneer sheet must be cut and
  applied with attention to grain direction. If the veneer's grain runs
  against the substrate, it buckles and cracks. A social veneer that
  contradicts the underlying personality too sharply is similarly
  unstable: the maintained performance creates visible stress at the
  points of greatest divergence.

- **Detection requires depth** -- you cannot tell veneer from solid wood
  by looking at the face. You must examine an edge, a cross-section, or
  wait for damage to expose the substrate. This maps onto the idea that
  social veneers are effective precisely because casual observation
  cannot penetrate them. Only sustained contact, crisis, or structural
  failure reveals the substrate.

## Limits

- **The source domain values veneer; the metaphor condemns it** -- in
  actual woodworking, veneering is a mark of skill, not fraud. Fine
  furniture has always been veneered. The technique allows beautiful but
  unstable woods (like burl) to be used over dimensionally stable
  substrates. Importing the word into social criticism smuggles in a
  judgment ("superficial deception") that the source domain does not
  support. This means the metaphor is doing persuasive work beyond what
  its structural mapping warrants.

- **Not all social surfaces are deceptive substrates** -- the metaphor
  assumes a knowable, stable "real self" hidden beneath a performed
  surface. But much of social life consists of performances that are
  not concealing anything in particular. Goffman's dramaturgical model
  suggests that the "front stage" is not a veneer over a hidden
  backstage self; it is a constitutive part of social identity. The
  metaphor's wood-over-wood ontology (two distinct layers, one real,
  one decorative) may not match the psychology.

- **Veneer does not peel casually** -- in the source domain, properly
  applied veneer is remarkably durable. It requires deliberate effort,
  moisture damage, or structural failure to delaminate. But the
  metaphorical usage implies fragility ("the veneer cracked," "the
  thin veneer peeled away"), overstating how easily social performances
  collapse. Most social veneers are quite robust under ordinary
  conditions.

- **The metaphor is dead enough to be invisible** -- most speakers using
  "veneer" have no awareness of the woodworking source. This means the
  structural constraints (grain direction, bonding pressure, species
  selection) that make the source domain analytically productive are
  lost. What remains is just "thin surface over hidden reality" -- a
  container metaphor with no carpentry-specific insight.

## Expressions

- "A thin veneer of civility" -- the most common collocation, implying
  that polite behavior barely conceals hostility or contempt
- "The veneer cracked" -- a moment when the maintained surface broke and
  the underlying reality became visible, used in journalism and political
  commentary
- "Strip away the veneer" -- to deliberately expose the reality beneath
  social performance, often framed as an act of courage or honesty
- "Veneer of respectability" -- applied to organizations or individuals
  whose public image conceals problematic practices
- "All veneer, no substance" -- used in product reviews, political
  criticism, and architectural criticism to describe something that is
  entirely surface

## Origin Story

Veneering as a craft technique dates to ancient Egypt; tomb fragments
show ebony veneer applied over local sycamore. The technique flourished
in 17th- and 18th-century European cabinet-making, where exotic woods
from colonial trade were too expensive and structurally unstable to use
as solid timber. The metaphorical sense appears in English by the early
18th century. Jonathan Swift used "veneer" figuratively in the 1720s.
By the 19th century, the metaphorical usage was thoroughly established
and the woodworking connection largely forgotten.
