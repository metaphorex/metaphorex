## Transfers

External events -- elections, seasons, wars, epidemics, construction
projects -- are understood as entities moving forward along a path. When
events proceed as expected, they "move forward" or "advance." When they
stall, they "stop in their tracks." When they reverse, they "go backward."
The metaphor is part of Lakoff and Johnson's Event Structure system,
specifically the location case: events are entities that travel along a
path, and the observer watches them approach, pass, and recede.

Key structural parallels:

- **Progress as forward motion** -- "The election is moving along."
  "Construction is advancing on schedule." "The peace talks are making
  headway." Events that proceed toward their expected completion are
  mapped onto objects moving forward along a path. This gives progress a
  spatial dimension: further along the path means closer to completion.
- **Stalling as cessation of motion** -- "The negotiations have ground to
  a halt." "The project isn't going anywhere." When external events stop
  progressing, the metaphor codes them as motionless objects. The
  implication is that something is blocking the path.
- **Regression as backward motion** -- "The situation has set us back."
  "We've lost ground on the epidemic." Events that worsen or reverse are
  mapped onto objects moving backward. This imposes a directional
  valuation: forward is good, backward is bad.
- **Speed as rate of development** -- "Events are moving fast." "The
  crisis is accelerating." "Reform has been slow." The rate at which
  events unfold is mapped onto the velocity of a moving body, giving
  observers a quantitative vocabulary for temporal development.
- **The observer as stationary** -- unlike ACTION IS MOTION, where the
  agent moves, this metaphor positions the observer as fixed and the
  events as moving. "The deadline is approaching." "The storm is bearing
  down on us." Events come to us; we do not go to them.

The metaphor is distinguished from ACTION IS MOTION by its external
perspective: the events are not the observer's own actions but things
happening in the world. The observer watches, tracks, and reacts to the
motion of events rather than producing it.

## Limits

- **Events are not on a single track** -- the metaphor imposes linearity
  on phenomena that are often multi-causal and multi-directional. A war
  can be "advancing" on one front and "retreating" on another
  simultaneously. An epidemic can be "surging" in one region and
  "receding" in another. The forward-motion metaphor forces a summary
  judgment (is it going forward or backward?) that collapses real
  complexity.
- **"Forward" smuggles in a value judgment** -- forward motion is
  implicitly good and backward motion implicitly bad. But some events
  progressing "forward" are catastrophic (a wildfire advancing, a war
  escalating). The metaphor makes it awkward to say that the forward
  progress of a bad thing is itself bad -- "the famine is advancing"
  sounds almost admiring.
- **The observer is not actually stationary** -- people are embedded in
  the events they describe. The metaphor's externality is an illusion
  that can produce false detachment. "Events are moving beyond our
  control" frames the speaker as a bystander when they may be a
  participant with agency.
- **Cyclic and recursive events break the metaphor** -- seasons,
  economic cycles, and recurring crises do not "move forward" in any
  meaningful sense. They recur. The metaphor has no natural vocabulary
  for recurrence -- only for linear advance and retreat.
- **The metaphor hides causation** -- by treating events as self-moving
  entities, the metaphor obscures who or what is causing them. "The war
  is advancing" elides the actors who are fighting it. The grammatical
  subject is the event, not the agents responsible for it.

## Expressions

- "Events are moving quickly" -- rate of development as velocity
- "The project is moving forward" -- progress as advance along a path
- "Things have come to a standstill" -- stalled events as motionless
  objects
- "The deadline is approaching" -- future events as entities moving
  toward the observer
- "The crisis has set us back years" -- regression as backward
  displacement
- "The storm is bearing down on us" -- an imminent event as an
  approaching mass
- "We need to stay ahead of events" -- maintaining a lead position
  relative to moving events
- "Events overtook us" -- events moving faster than the observer can
  respond

## Origin Story

This metaphor appears in the Master Metaphor List (Lakoff, Espenson,
and Schwartz, 1991) as part of the Event Structure metaphor system.
It belongs to what Lakoff and Johnson later termed the "moving time"
variant of time/event metaphors, where events and times are moving
entities and the observer is stationary -- as opposed to the "moving
observer" variant, where the observer travels through time. The two
variants are complementary and coexist in English: "the weeks ahead"
(moving observer) versus "the coming weeks" (moving time). This entry
covers the moving-event variant as applied specifically to external
events and their progress.

The metaphor is grounded in the embodied experience of watching objects
approach and recede -- a ball rolling toward you, a person walking away.
The infant's experience of events-as-approaching-objects (a parent's face
getting closer, a sound getting louder) provides the sensorimotor basis
for the conceptual mapping.
