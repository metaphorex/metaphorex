## Transfers

Napoleon's Maxim VI states: "When once the offensive has been assumed,
it must be sustained to the last extremity." This is not a counsel of
blind aggression. It is a structural observation about the economics
of initiative: going on offense is expensive to start and expensive
to sustain, but cheapest to sustain once started, because pausing
transfers initiative to the opponent and wastes the investment already
made in creating momentum.

Key structural parallels:

- **Initiative is perishable.** In Napoleonic warfare, an offensive
  creates a specific condition: the enemy is reacting to you rather
  than you to them. This condition degrades the moment you stop
  advancing. The defender uses every pause to reinforce, reposition,
  and prepare. The structural transfer is that initiative is not a
  possession but a flow state -- it exists only while you are acting.
  In product competition, a company that launches aggressively and
  then pauses for "consolidation" gives competitors time to copy,
  respond, and reframe. In litigation, a plaintiff who files and then
  delays discovery allows the defense to build its case. The maxim
  says: once you have decided to act, the cost of maintaining momentum
  is lower than the cost of losing it and having to recreate it.

- **Half-measures spend full costs for partial results.** Launching an
  offensive expends resources, reveals intentions, and commits
  reputation. These costs are paid whether the offensive succeeds or
  stalls. A half-hearted offensive pays the political and material
  costs of aggression without achieving the strategic objective.
  Napoleon's insight is that the worst position is the middle: having
  spent enough to provoke a response but not enough to achieve a
  decision. In business, this is the product launch with insufficient
  marketing, the acquisition with inadequate integration planning, or
  the organizational restructuring that stops halfway -- each paying
  the disruption costs without achieving the intended benefits.

- **Sustained pressure exploits the defender's finite reserves.** An
  offensive that continues without pause forces the defender to
  maintain maximum readiness continuously, which is exhausting and
  unsustainable. The attacker chooses when and where to press; the
  defender must be ready everywhere. In competitive strategy, this
  translates to rapid iteration cycles that force competitors to
  respond continuously -- each response costing them resources and
  attention that cannot be spent on their own initiatives.

- **The decision to go on offense should be made once, not
  continuously.** The maxim implicitly argues against re-litigating
  the decision to attack at every stage. Once the strategic
  assessment has been made and the offensive launched, the question
  shifts from "should we be doing this?" to "how do we sustain
  this?" The former question, asked mid-offensive, produces
  hesitation that is operationally indistinguishable from retreat.
  In organizational terms, this is the difference between a committed
  strategy with defined exit criteria and a tentative initiative that
  gets reviewed to death in weekly status meetings.

## Limits

- **It is a sunk-cost accelerator when the premise is wrong.** The
  maxim assumes the original decision to go on offense was correct.
  When it was not -- when the intelligence was wrong, the market
  was misread, the enemy was stronger than estimated -- sustaining
  the offensive converts a recoverable error into a catastrophe.
  Napoleon himself demonstrated this limit at Moscow in 1812: the
  offensive was sustained to the last extremity, and the result was
  the destruction of the Grande Armee. The maxim provides no
  structural test for distinguishing justified persistence from
  sunk-cost escalation.

- **It assumes unified command.** Napoleon could sustain an offensive
  because he controlled the army. Most modern offensives -- product
  launches, market expansions, organizational transformations --
  require sustained buy-in from boards, investors, partners, and
  employees. The maxim has no structural place for coalition
  maintenance. A startup founder who "sustains the offensive" while
  investors lose confidence and engineers quit is not being
  Napoleonic; they are being delusional about their command authority.

- **It privileges attack over defense.** The maxim treats the
  offensive as inherently superior to the defensive, but military
  history is full of successful defensive strategies: Wellington at
  Torres Vedras, Kutuzov's retreat before Moscow, the Finnish
  defense in the Winter War. In business, defensive strategies --
  protecting market share, deepening customer relationships,
  strengthening operations -- can be more value-creating than
  offensive expansion. The maxim's bias toward action can cause
  organizations to attack when they should be fortifying.

- **"Last extremity" is undefined.** The maxim says to sustain the
  offensive but provides no metric for when you have reached the
  extremity. In practice, this means the maxim is invoked to justify
  one more push, one more quarter, one more sprint -- indefinitely.
  Without a pre-committed definition of the last extremity, the
  maxim degenerates into "never stop," which is not strategy but
  stubbornness.

## Expressions

- "When once the offensive has been assumed, it must be sustained to
  the last extremity" -- the full maxim, Maxim VI in standard
  collections of Napoleon's military maxims
- "Maintain the initiative" -- the military doctrine derivative,
  used in business strategy to mean continuous forward movement
- "Never let up" -- the colloquial business compression, losing the
  original conditional structure
- "Don't give them time to breathe" -- competitive strategy
  formulation used in product management and sales
- "Sustained offensive" -- used in technology and business journalism
  to describe continuous competitive pressure (product releases,
  feature shipping, market expansion)
- "Losing momentum" -- the negative form, describing what happens when
  the maxim is violated, used across sports, business, and military
  contexts

## Origin Story

The maxim appears in collections of Napoleon's military observations,
most authoritatively compiled from his correspondence and the memoirs
of his marshals. Napoleon's operational genius was characterized by
speed and sustained pressure: the Ulm campaign of 1805 and the
Austerlitz campaign that followed demonstrated what sustained
offensive momentum could achieve against numerically superior
coalitions.

The maxim was not abstract theory for Napoleon -- it was learned from
the campaigns of Frederick the Great and validated in his own Italian
campaigns of 1796-1797, where a smaller French force defeated larger
Austrian and Sardinian armies through relentless tempo. The principle
was later studied extensively by Clausewitz (who fought against
Napoleon) and became a foundational concept in nineteenth-century
military theory.

The irony is that Napoleon's greatest failure -- the 1812 Russian
campaign -- is the maxim taken to its logical extreme. He sustained
the offensive to Moscow and beyond, refusing to pause, consolidate,
or retreat, and the result was the destruction of the largest army
Europe had seen. The maxim's own author provides its most devastating
counterexample, suggesting that "to the last extremity" requires a
definition of extremity that Napoleon himself did not observe.
