## Transfers

Authority as a physical chain: each link connected to the next, transmitting
force from the top to the point of application at the bottom. The metaphor is
so deeply embedded in organizational language that it has become invisible --
"chain of command" is treated as a literal description of organizational
structure rather than as a metaphor that imports specific assumptions from
metallurgy and mechanics.

Key structural parallels:

- **Sequential transmission** -- in a chain, force passes through every link
  in order. You cannot pull the fifth link without pulling the fourth. In
  organizational terms: orders must flow through every level of the hierarchy.
  A general does not give orders directly to a private; the order passes
  through colonels, majors, captains, and lieutenants. This is not
  inefficiency but design: each intermediary translates the order into the
  context of their level. The chain metaphor frames this as a feature, not
  a bug.
- **Weakest-link failure** -- a chain's strength is determined by its weakest
  link. This maps directly onto the observation that one incompetent manager
  in a hierarchy can block information flow and distort authority for everyone
  below them. The metaphor gives organizations language for a specific failure
  mode: the problem is not the chain's design but the quality of individual
  links.
- **No lateral connections** -- a chain is one-dimensional. Links connect
  only to the link above and below, never sideways. This maps onto the strict
  prohibition against "going around" your superior -- a violation that
  military organizations treat as a serious breach of protocol. The metaphor
  frames lateral communication as structurally impossible, which reinforces
  hierarchical discipline but can prevent information from reaching the
  people who need it.
- **Tension and load** -- a chain under tension distributes load evenly across
  all links. In organizational terms, this maps onto the principle that
  accountability flows upward: every link bears the weight of the decisions
  made above it. A commander is responsible for everything their subordinates
  do, because the chain connects them. Accountability, like tension, cannot
  skip links.

## Limits

- **Chains do not filter or interpret** -- a steel chain transmits exactly the
  force applied to it. Human chains of command transform information at every
  level. Bad news is softened, context is stripped, urgency is amplified or
  dampened. By the time information reaches the top -- or orders reach the
  bottom -- the original signal may be unrecognizable. The metaphor's
  assumption of faithful transmission is its most dangerous structural failure.
- **The metaphor prohibits necessary bypass** -- in crisis situations, strict
  adherence to the chain of command can be fatal. A soldier who sees an
  imminent threat needs to act or communicate directly, not pass a message
  through four levels of hierarchy. Modern military doctrine acknowledges
  this with concepts like "mission command" and "commander's intent," which
  deliberately loosen the chain. The metaphor itself offers no vocabulary
  for authorized bypass.
- **Chains are rigid; organizations must flex** -- a chain that bends is
  still a chain, but it cannot branch, merge, or reconfigure. Organizations
  routinely need matrix reporting, cross-functional teams, and temporary
  authority structures. The chain metaphor frames all of these as deviations
  from the proper structure rather than as legitimate organizational forms.
- **It naturalizes hierarchy** -- by framing authority as a physical object
  (a chain), the metaphor makes hierarchical organization seem like a
  natural law rather than a design choice. This discourages questioning
  whether the hierarchy itself is the right structure for the task at hand.
  A network, a market, or a consensus process might serve better, but the
  chain metaphor makes these alternatives literally unthinkable -- there is
  no chain-shaped version of a network.

## Expressions

- "Go through the chain of command" -- directive to follow hierarchical
  protocol rather than communicating directly
- "Don't break the chain" -- warning against bypassing intermediate
  authority
- "The chain of command is only as strong as its weakest link" -- diagnosing
  a specific management failure
- "That's above my pay grade" -- locating oneself within the chain and
  deferring upward
- "Short-circuiting the chain of command" -- describing unauthorized direct
  communication, borrowing from electrical metaphors to describe the
  organizational violation

## Origin Story

The phrase "chain of command" entered English military usage in the early
19th century, though the hierarchical structure it describes is ancient.
The metaphor draws on the physical chain -- a technology whose properties
(sequential linkage, weakest-link failure, uni-directional force
transmission) mapped naturally onto military organization. The Prussian
military reforms of the early 1800s formalized the hierarchical staff
structure that the metaphor describes, and the terminology spread to
English-speaking armies through military education.

The metaphor jumped to civilian organizations in the early 20th century as
military veterans entered corporate management and brought their vocabulary
with them. Frederick Taylor's scientific management and Henri Fayol's
administrative theory both assumed chain-of-command structures, and the
metaphor became the default model for corporate hierarchy. Its dominance
began to erode only in the 1980s and 1990s with flat organizations, matrix
structures, and agile methodologies -- all of which are defined partly by
their rejection of the chain metaphor.

## References

- Fayol, H. *General and Industrial Management* (1916; English translation
  1949) -- codified the "scalar chain" principle in management theory
- Van Creveld, M. *Command in War* (1985) -- historical analysis of
  military command structures and their information-processing limits
- McChrystal, S. *Team of Teams* (2015) -- argues for replacing chain-of-
  command with network-of-teams, explicitly critiquing the chain metaphor
